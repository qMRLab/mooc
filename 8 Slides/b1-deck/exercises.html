Far below the Ernst angle the image scales like B1 (as a receive field would). Well above it, the steady state and the trigonometry pull in opposite directions; they balance at one angle, where the image scales like 1/B1. That window closes as TR grows: at long TR, S = sin(αB1) is never like 1/B1.

Bloch–Siegert · (a) What we saw

Far off resonance, the pulse barely tips M

  • In the frame turning with the RF, the spins feel a static effective field: B1 along x′, plus −Δω/γ along z, what is left of B0 in that frame.
  • 4 kHz off, Δω/γ = 0.94 G while B1 peaks at 0.1 G: Beff leans only 6° from −z. M along z circles it in a narrow cone, and since the pulse rises and falls slowly, M follows Beff back to z: it ends tipped by 0.03°.
  • On resonance the same pulse turns M by about 870°; at 250 Hz Beff leans 60° and M ends far from z. A Bloch–Siegert pulse must be far enough off resonance to excite nothing, including spins at their own B0 offset.

Bloch–Siegert · (b) What we saw

A frequency shift while the pulse is on

  • Frame of the RF: M circles Beff at ωeff = √(Δω² + ω1²), a little faster than Δω.
  • Larmor frame: that frame itself turns at Δω, so all that is left is the difference, ωBS = √(Δω² + ω1²) − Δω ≈ ω1²/2Δω. M stays in the transverse plane (|Mxy| = 1) and turns about z, away from the RF frequency.
  • Over the pulse it gains φBS = ∫ω1(t)²/2Δω dt = KBS·B1²: about 42° here. The magnitude image is untouched; B1 is written into the phase.

Bloch–Siegert · (c) What we saw

φBS = KBS·B1², with KBS ∝ 1/offset

  • Quadratic in B1: twice the B1, four times the phase. A 10 % error in B1 gives 21 % more phase, so the phase is a sensitive measure of B1.
  • Inversely proportional to the offset: 2 kHz gives twice the phase of 4 kHz. But closer to resonance the pulse starts to tip M (part a), and the B1² law bends: the exact shift is √(Δω² + ω1²) − Δω, which falls below ω1²/2Δω when ω1 is not ≪ Δω (points under the dashed curve).
  • KBS = γ²∫b(t)²dt / 2Δω depends only on the pulse shape b(t) and the offset, so it is known in advance (74 rad/G² for this pulse at 4 kHz). The price is SAR: a long, strong pulse.

Bloch–Siegert · (d) What we saw

The difference keeps B1 and drops everything else

  • Each spin's phase is φ0 + 2π·δf·T ∓ KBSB1²: the starting phase (excitation, receive coil), its B0 offset over the pulse, and the Bloch–Siegert shift, whose sign follows the side of the offset.
  • Subtracting the two acquisitions cancels φ0 and the B0 term: (φ− − φ+)/2 = KBSB1² for every spin, to 0.1 % even at ±100 Hz. Then B1 = √((φ− − φ+)/2KBS).
  • The magnitude images are the same in both acquisitions, and neither T1 nor the flip angle of the excitation enters: unlike the double angle method or AFI, the B1 information is in a phase difference.

Bloch–Siegert · Lessons

What the spins showed

  • Far off resonance, a strong pulse excites nothing: the spins see an effective field almost along z.
  • While it is on, transverse spins precess a little faster, ωBS ≈ ω1²/2Δω, so they gain a phase φBS = KBSB1², quadratic in B1.
  • Repeating with the offset on the other side flips the sign of φBS and nothing else: the phase difference gives B1, free of B0, of the receive phase and of T1.
  • The approximation ω1²/2Δω holds when ω1 ≪ Δω: a large offset keeps it exact and avoids excitation, at the cost of less phase per unit SAR.
