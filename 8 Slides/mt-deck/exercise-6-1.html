Two tanks, one pipe

In this exercise, we will cover:

  1. The model: MT as two tanks, and what each part stands for
  2. Predict, then watch: one pool, then two pools without exchange, then with exchange
  3. The two-pool equations as plumbing: level, width, faucet and drain, one pipe
  4. What the off-resonance RF drains, and how exchange passes it on to water
  5. What changes with no exchange, with no macromolecules, and with the RF close to resonance

The model first, then predict and watch in twelve quick questions, then play with the RF, the sliders and the presets.

The model: MT as two tanks

  1. Faucet and drain = T1. Each tank refills toward full and leaks in proportion to its level; they balance when the tank is full, at equilibrium (M0).
  2. RF pulse = an extra drain. How far it opens depends on how far the RF is from that pool’s resonance, compared with the width of its line: fully open on resonance, closing as the offset grows. Water’s line is narrow (T2 ≈ 37 ms), so its drain is nearly shut a few hundred Hz away; the macromolecules’ line is very broad (T2 ≈ 12 µs), so theirs stays open tens of kHz away. An off-resonance pulse drains mostly the macromolecules.
  3. Exchange = a pipe between the tanks. It flows from the higher level to the lower one, in either direction, faster for a bigger difference (kf). It moves Mz without creating or destroying it.
  4. Level = Mz/M0 of that pool; width = pool size. The macromolecule tank is F ≈ 16 % as wide, so its level moves 1/F ≈ 6× faster for the same flow (kr = kf/F).
  5. We only see the water. The macromolecules’ signal decays in microseconds, so the image reads the water level: MT shows up as a lower water tank.
What the analogy maps, before the questions. The longitudinal two-pool (Bloch–McConnell) equations: dMz,f/dt = R1f(M0f − Mz,f) − k_f Mz,f + k_r Mz,r − W_f Mz,f, and the same for the restricted pool with W_r. Level = Mz/M0 of each pool; width = pool size, so the macromolecule tank (F = M0r/M0f ≈ 16 % in WM) holds 16 % as much. Faucet + drain = T1: constant inflow R1·M0 against an outflow R1·Mz, balancing at M0. The RF drain opens at W = π ω1² G(Δ), G the pool's absorption lineshape at the RF offset Δ: water's Lorentzian (T2,f ≈ 37 ms) is a few Hz wide, the macromolecules' super-Lorentzian (T2,r ≈ 12 µs) tens of kHz, so at a typical 1–2 kHz offset the RF drains the macromolecules and leaves water nearly alone (direct saturation). On resonance both drains are wide open (a 90° pulse also saturates part of the macromolecular pool). Exchange (dipolar cross-relaxation, chemical exchange) is a pipe whose flow follows the level difference, k_f(h_f − h_r) per unit of water; the same volume leaving the wide tank enters the narrow one, so the narrow level moves 1/F times faster, k_r = k_f/F. Only longitudinal magnetization is drawn; the imaging readout measures the water tank's level. The inset's lines are schematic, not to scale (the real water line is far narrower).

Predict, then watch: from one pool to MT

  1. Water only. An on-resonance 90° pulse hits it at 1 s. Right after the pulse, Mz/M0 is… A 90° pulse tips all of Mz into the transverse plane, so Mz = M0 cos 90° = 0. From there it grows back with T1.
  2. With T1 = 1 s, how long after the pulse until Mz is back to about 95 % of M0? Mz = M0(1 − e−t/T1): 63 % after one T1, 95 % after three.
  3. Now a long off-resonance pulse, 1.5 kHz away from water, left on for 5 s: long enough to reach a steady state. The water level… Water’s line is narrow (T2 ≈ 37 ms, a few Hz wide), so it absorbs very little 1.5 kHz away: it settles at only R1/(R1 + Wf) ≈ 0.84. This is direct saturation.
  4. Add the macromolecules, but no exchange. The same long off-resonance pulse. The macromolecules… Their protons are locked in place, so their T2 is tiny (≈ 12 µs) and their line is tens of kHz wide: at 1.5 kHz they absorb strongly and settle at about 0.04.
  5. And the water, in this same experiment? Without exchange, water can’t tell the macromolecules are there: it only gets direct saturation, the same 0.84 as with water alone. Each pool recovers with its own T1.
  6. Now exchange, no RF, and imagine no relaxation at all (T1 switched off). The macromolecule pool is only F = 16 % the size of the water pool (its tank is about 6× narrower). The water is full; the macromolecules are at 0.2. Where do the levels end up? The pipe flows from high to low until the levels match. The narrow tank holds only 16 % as much Mz, so it fills fast while water drops a little: they meet at the volume-weighted mean (1 + 0.161 × 0.2)/1.161 ≈ 0.89, in tens of ms (shown in slow motion), and stay there: with no relaxation, nothing else changes the total. Exchange moves Mz; it doesn’t create or destroy it.
  7. The opposite case, still with no relaxation: the water is saturated (0.2) and the macromolecules are full. Same pool sizes (F = 16 %). Where do the levels end up? The pipe now flows the other way, but the narrow tank has little to give: it empties into the water and raises it only a little. They meet at (0.2 + 0.161 × 1)/1.161 ≈ 0.31 and stay there. The macromolecules can’t refill the water; only T1 can (back on in the next questions).
  8. Both pools full. What does exchange do? Equal levels, no net flow. Spins still swap between the pools all the time, but as many go each way, so nothing changes.
  9. Relaxation is back on. Both pools equally low, at 0.3. What happens? Exchange only acts on a difference in level; it can’t add Mz. Only T1 refills them, together (0.3 → 0.58 in the 0.5 s shown).
  10. Now the same long pulse (5 s, to steady state) with exchange. The water settles… The RF keeps the macromolecules below the water level, and exchange keeps passing the saturation on until RF draining, exchange and T1 balance: water settles at about 0.32, far below direct saturation alone (0.84). That extra drop is the MT effect, and with a pulse this long it is reached during the pulse: once the RF stops, both pools recover.
  11. And the macromolecules: compared with no exchange (0.04), is their steady-state level lower or higher? Higher, about 0.18: while the RF drains them, water keeps refilling them through the pipe. The saturation is shared, not created.
  12. Now a short, strong off-resonance pulse (20 ms): it saturates the macromolecules before the water has time to respond. Once the RF stops, the water… Keeps dropping: exchange doesn’t stop with the RF. At the end of the pulse the macromolecules (0.14) are far below the water (0.92), so the pipe keeps flowing, and the water drops to about 0.83 some 0.1 s later, when the levels meet; then both recover with T1. With short pulses, as in MT-prepared sequences, much of the MT effect happens after the pulse.
A predict-first quiz that builds the two-pool model up before the full plumbing on the next slide. Each answer plays a short longitudinal Bloch–McConnell simulation (healthy WM, Sled & Pike 2001: F = 16.1 %, k_f = 4.3 s⁻¹, so k_r = k_f/F ≈ 27 s⁻¹; R1f = R1r = 1 s⁻¹). Stage 1, water only: an on-resonance 90° pulse (instantaneous here) zeroes Mz, which regrows with T1 (95 % after 3 s); a long off-resonance pulse (3 µT continuous wave, 1.5 kHz, 5 s, long enough to reach steady state) dips it only to about 0.84 (W_f ≈ 0.2 s⁻¹, Lorentzian, T2,f = 37 ms): direct saturation. Stage 2, macromolecules added with no exchange: the same pulse saturates them to about 0.04 (super-Lorentzian, T2,r = 12.3 µs, W_r ≈ 25 s⁻¹) and the water is unchanged. Stage 3, exchange on its own in slow motion (×10, the levels even out in tens of ms), first with relaxation switched off so that only exchange acts: from water full and macromolecules at 0.2 they meet at the volume-weighted mean ≈ 0.89; in the opposite case (water at 0.2, macromolecules full) at ≈ 0.31: the small pool barely lifts the water. Then with T1 back on: equal levels, high or low, give no net flow. Then the long pulse with exchange: at steady state the water sits at about 0.32, the macromolecules at about 0.18; with a pulse this long the MT effect is complete during the pulse, so both recover as soon as it stops. Last, a short strong pulse (8 µT, 20 ms) saturates the macromolecules (0.14) before water responds (0.92 at the end of the pulse): exchange keeps going after the RF, and the water keeps dropping, to about 0.83 roughly 0.1 s later (1/(k_f + k_r) ≈ 32 ms), before recovering. With short pulses, as in MT-prepared sequences, much of the MT effect happens after the pulse. The difference between stages 2 and 3 is the MT effect.

Two tanks, one pipe

A general simulator of the two-tank model: set the RF, the pool size, the exchange and T1, and watch both levels. Height = Mz/M0, width = pool size, faucet + drain = T1, pipe = exchange, valves = RF.

dhf/dt = R1f(1 − hf) − kf(hf − hr) − Wfhf
dhr/dt = R1r(1 − hr) + (kf/F)(hf − hr) − Wrhr

hf = Mz,f/M0,f, the water level · hr = Mz,r/M0,r, the macromolecule level (1 = full, at equilibrium)

The two-pool longitudinal equations (Eqs. 6.1–6.5, continuous-wave RF) drawn as plumbing. Water level h = Mz/M0 of each pool; tank width = pool size, so the macromolecule tank is F as wide and holds F of the magnetization (Mz,f = h_f, Mz,r = F·h_r). Faucet + drain = T1 relaxation: the lattice's net bias toward alignment (constant inflow R1·M0) against thermal scrambling (outflow R1·Mz); they balance at M0. One pipe at the bottom flows with the height difference: the same volume leaves one tank and enters the other, so the narrow tank's level moves 1/F times faster, which is k_r = k_f/F, built in; substituting gives −k_f Mz,f + k_r Mz,r. Exchange (dipolar flip-flops, chemical exchange) conserves Mz, as the pipe conserves water; only the lattice (T1) and the RF add or remove it. The RF valve drains the macromolecule tank at W_r = π ω1² G(Δ) (super-Lorentzian, T2,r = 12.3 µs); the small valve on the water tank is direct saturation, W_f (Lorentzian, T2,f = 37 ms): bring the offset close to see it grow. Defaults: healthy white matter (Sled & Pike 2001), 2.3 µT at 2 kHz, giving an MTR of about 56 % at steady state. The macromolecule T1 is fixed at 1 s. Each visit starts a 4 s pulse 1 s in.

Exercise 6-1 · Summary

Summary

  1. Each pool is a tank: its level is Mz/M0, its width the pool size; T1 refills both toward full.
  2. The off-resonance RF drains only the narrow macromolecule tank; the pipe (exchange) lowers water with it. That drop in water is the MT effect.
  3. Without exchange, water does not notice; without macromolecules, only direct saturation is left, small unless the RF is close to resonance.
