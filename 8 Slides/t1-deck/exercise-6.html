A collaborator’s strange T1 values

You receive an email from a collaborator in your city asking for help with a T1 dataset they acquired on their new 3T scanner. They tried fitting the data with qMRLab already, but are getting T1 values in the brain that are beyond the expected values (eg, WM: 5000 ms, GM: 5000 ms). They send you their acquired data.

  1. a. Fit a T1 map using their data and plot a few voxel signal curves.
  2. b. Luckily, you own a standardized quantitative MRI NIST phantom with known T1 values, and are able to go to your collaborators scanner to acquire a dataset using their protocol. Here is data you acquired for a region of the phantom with a known T1 value (987 ms)* close to the expected WM values. Plot that data, and simulate the expected signal curve for this T1 value
  3. c. Comparing the human and phantom data and the simulated curves, what do you notice? What do you think may be the problem?
  4. d. Test your proposed problem with some of your simulation code.
  5. e. What would you propose to your collaborator? Is a new data acquisition protocol required, or can you provide some post-processing corrections prior to fitting the data?

* Data: ISMRM 2020 T1 mapping challenge, one brain and one NIST phantom scanned on the same GE 3T (TR 2550 ms), resampled to 10 TIs from 50 to 2500 ms. Fits run live in this page with qmrust.

Exercise 6 from the end of the T1 chapter, quoted as written. The data come from subject 01 and the NIST phantom (serial 46) of the USC site of the ISMRM 2020 T1 mapping challenge (osf.io/ywc9g), 2D spin-echo IR, TR 2550 ms, acquired at TI 50, 400, 1100 and 2500 ms. Each voxel was fitted and its signal resampled to 10 TIs equispaced from 50 to 2500 ms, with noise matching the real images (tools/build_ex6_data.py). The fault, kept for the reveal: the images were saved in the reverse order of the TIs listed in the header.

a. Fit their data

a. Fit a T1 map using their data.

Every voxel comes out at 5000 ms, the upper limit of the fit, WM and GM alike. Expected at 3T: WM ≈ 0.9 s, GM ≈ 1.5 s (Figure 2.2). The fit has failed everywhere.

The slide opens on the ten images with the TIs their header gives. Click 1: qmrust fits every brain voxel (general-equation RD-NLS, magnitude, as qMRLab's inversion_recovery), about 37,000 voxels in two or three seconds. The table gives the median T1 in four ROIs from the challenge analysis. Click 2: every voxel sits at 5000 ms, the fit's upper bound (qMRLab's too): the fit fails, it is not a tissue property.

a. Voxel signal curves

a. … and plot a few voxel signal curves.

The fits miss the data: the signal falls from the shortest TI, dips, then rises again. No inversion recovery curve does that. To find out why, we need data whose T1 we know.

The slide opens on the mean signal of each ROI against the header TIs. Click 1: the fitted curves (qmrust, general equation), both stuck at T1 = 5000 ms; they cannot follow a signal that starts high, dips near 2000 ms and recovers. Click 2: something is wrong with the data or the protocol; a reference with known T1 is needed, which is part b.

b. The NIST phantom, same scanner and protocol

b. Plot the data from a phantom region with a known T1, and simulate the expected signal curve for this T1.

The slide opens on the phantom image, with sphere 3 circled: NIST reference T1 987 ms at 20 °C (phantom serial 46). Click 1: its mean signal against the header TIs. Click 2: the expected signal from the general equation (Eq. 2.2, TR 2550 ms, magnitude), computed by qmrust. Data and simulation are each divided by their maximum, so only the shapes are compared.

c. What do you notice?

c. Comparing the human and phantom data and the simulated curves, what do you notice? What do you think may be the problem?

The data look like the simulation read backwards: the images may be stored in the reverse order of the TIs in the header.

Human WM, the phantom sphere and the simulation, all normalized to their maximum. Click 1: the simulation's dip (its null) is near 594 ms; the data dip late (phantom near 1956 ms, WM near 2228 ms) and fall from the shortest TI where an inversion recovery should be rising. Mirror the simulation left to right and it has the data's shape. Click 2: the hypothesis: the images are in reverse TI order.

d. Test the hypothesis

d. Test your proposed problem with some of your simulation code.

The slide opens on the phantom data and the simulation, as in part b. Click 1: the simulated values taken in reverse TI order follow the measured pattern; the leftover differences (e.g. at the longest TI) come from the real, imperfect inversion pulse, which the fit's free a and b parameters absorb. Click 2: fitting the phantom spheres with the images in reverse order recovers the NIST reference values; as delivered every sphere fits to 5000 ms, the bound.

e. What to tell the collaborator

e. Is a new data acquisition protocol required, or can you provide some post-processing corrections prior to fitting the data?

No new acquisition. Reorder the images by the TI stored in each image’s own header, then refit. Check the result on the phantom.

The slide opens on the collaborator's T1 map. Click 1: the same data with the ten images in reverse order, refitted by qmrust: WM about 0.85–0.92 s and GM about 1.25–1.39 s, as expected at 3T. Click 2: the proposal. The images were probably sorted by something other than TI (series number, file name), so the TI list and the images no longer matched; the data themselves are fine.
