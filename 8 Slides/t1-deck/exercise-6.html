A collaborator’s strange T1 values

You receive an email from a collaborator in your city asking for help with a T1 dataset they acquired on their new 3T scanner. They tried fitting the data with qMRLab already, but are getting T1 values in the brain that are beyond the expected values (eg, WM: 2000ms, GM, 5000 ms).* They send you their acquired data.

  1. a. Fit a T1 map using their data and plot a few voxel signal curves.
  2. b. Luckily, you own a standardized quantitative MRI NIST phantom with known T1 values, and are able to go to your collaborators scanner to acquire a dataset using their protocol. Here is data you acquired for a region of the phantom with a known T1 value (900 ms +- 5ms)* close to the expected WM values. Plot that data, and simulate the expected signal curve for this T1 value
  3. c. Comparing the human and phantom data and the simulated curves, what do you notice? What do you think may be the problem?
  4. d. Test your proposed problem with some of your simulation code.
  5. e. What would you propose to your collaborator? Is a new data acquisition protocol required, or can you provide some post-processing corrections prior to fitting the data?

* Data: ISMRM 2020 T1 mapping challenge, one brain and one NIST phantom scanned on the same GE 3T with the same IR protocol (TR 2550 ms). With these data the collaborator’s fit gives WM ≈ 1.6–1.7 s with GM below WM, and the phantom sphere’s reference T1 is 987 ms. Fits run live in this page with qmrust.

The data are real: subject 01 and the NIST phantom (serial 46) from the USC site of the ISMRM 2020 T1 mapping challenge (osf.io/ywc9g), 2D spin-echo IR, TR 2550 ms, TI 50, 400, 1100, 2500 ms. The fault, kept for the reveal: the images were saved in the reverse order of the TIs listed in the header.

a. Voxel signal curves

a. … and plot a few voxel signal curves.

The fits go through the points: nothing looks wrong in the brain data on their own. To find the problem, we need data whose T1 we know.

The slide opens on the mean signal of each ROI against the header TIs. Click 1: the fitted curves (qmrust, general equation). c. What do you notice?

c. Comparing the human and phantom data and the simulated curves, what do you notice? What do you think may be the problem?

The data look like the simulation read backwards: the images may be stored in the reverse order of the TIs in the header.

Human WM, the phantom sphere and the simulation, all normalized to their maximum. Click 1: the simulation's dip (its null) is at 400 ms; both data sets dip at 1100 ms and are highest at the shortest TI, where an inversion recovery should be most negative. Click 2: the hypothesis: the images are in reverse TI order.

d. Test the hypothesis

d. Test your proposed problem with some of your simulation code.

The slide opens on the phantom data and the simulation, as in part b. Click 1: the simulated values taken in reverse TI order follow the measured pattern; the leftover differences (e.g. at the longest TI) come from the real, imperfect inversion pulse, which the fit's free a and b parameters absorb. Click 2: fitting the phantom spheres with the images in reverse order recovers the NIST reference values; as delivered they are far off.

e. What to tell the collaborator

e. Is a new data acquisition protocol required, or can you provide some post-processing corrections prior to fitting the data?

No new acquisition. Reorder the images by the TI stored in each image’s own header, then refit. Check the result on the phantom.

The slide opens on the collaborator's T1 map. Click 1: the same data with the four images in reverse order, refitted by qmrust: WM about 0.87–0.91 s and GM about 1.25–1.39 s, WM now shorter than GM. Click 2: the proposal. The images were probably sorted by something other than TI (series number, file name), so the TI list and the images no longer matched; the data themselves are fine.
