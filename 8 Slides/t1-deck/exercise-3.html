Avoid relying on TIs around the null: the signal is close to zero there, so noise, and the long-TR model’s offset e−TR/T1, are relatively largest, and errors at those TIs move the fitted T1 the most.

The slide opens on the data: WM at 1.5 T, TR = 3 s, sampled every 100 ms (general equation, magnitude, by qmrust). Click 1: add a small error (1% of M0, like noise) to one TI at a time, refit with the long-TR model, and plot how far the fitted T1 moves: the bottom panel. Click 2: the TIs where an error moves T1 the most, shaded: around the null (≈ 444 ms) and up to about T1. Click 3: why to avoid them. Errors at TIs far past the null hardly move T1: those points carry little T1 information, which the next slide shows. The shifts are small here (about 1 ms) because 29 TIs share the load; with a 4-TI protocol the same error moves T1 about five times more.

c. A protocol that samples only there

Three TIs, 100 ms apart, slid along the recovery. Each position is fitted with the long-TR model, whose offset e−TR/T1 acts as an error at every TI (noise-free data, WM at 1.5 T, TR = 3 s).

Avoid protocols that sample mostly around the null (≈ 450 ms), where errors move T1 the most, or far past it (≳ 2.5 T1), where the curve is flat and carries no T1 information. Spread the TIs across the recovery, and keep TI below TR minus the readout.

Three clicks. 1: the sweep: each point is a long-TR fit by qmrust to three TIs (centre ± 100 ms) of noise-free data. The only error is the long-TR model's own offset. 2: the problem zones, marked. Near the null, where the previous slide showed T1 is most sensitive to errors, the fit error swings to ±20–30%; from about 1.6 s on, the three samples lie on the plateau and the fit cannot find T1 at all (it runs to its bounds). Spread-out protocols keep the bias near the 1.7% of part b. 3: the answer.
