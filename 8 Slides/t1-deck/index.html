Why map T1?

  • T1, the spin–lattice relaxation time: the rate at which excited spins return to equilibrium with their surroundings
  • A quantitative biomarker
  • Needed to optimize protocols, e.g. the Ernst angle for maximum SNR in spoiled gradient echo (Ernst 1966)
  • A correction factor for other qMRI techniques: quantitative MT (Cercignani 2005; Yarnykh 2002) and DCE (Li 2018; Sung 2013)
T1 characterizes the rate at which excited nuclear spins return to equilibrium with their environment. Beyond its value as a biomarker, accurate T1 knowledge is essential for optimizing imaging protocols, such as computing the Ernst angle. T1 values also serve as correction factors in qMT and DCE, making T1 mapping a crucial part of comprehensive qMRI protocols.

Three approaches in this chapter

Inversion recovery

Samples the recovery of Mz after an inversion pulse.

Gold standard, but needs TR of 2–5 T1, so whole-organ maps are hard in clinical times.

Variable flip angle

Several spoiled gradient echo images with different flip angles.

Rapid 3D, but sensitive to flip-angle errors, so it needs a B1 map.

MP2RAGE

Dictionary-based; combines elements of IR and VFA.

Turnkey and increasingly available on clinical scanners.

Inversion recovery is widely considered the gold standard (Stikov 2015) but requires long TRs (Steen 1994). VFA offers rapid 3D T1 mapping but is sensitive to flip angle inaccuracies from transmit field inhomogeneities, so it needs B1 mapping (Sled 1998, Boudreau 2017). Dictionary-based approaches like MP2RAGE combine elements of both and use pre-calculated dictionaries; their availability on clinical scanners makes quantitative T1 mapping increasingly accessible.

Method 1 · Inversion recovery

Inversion Recovery T1 Mapping

Widely considered the gold standard for T1 mapping

Inversion recovery estimates T1 by fitting the signal recovery curve acquired at different delays after an inversion pulse (180°).

The inversion recovery experiment

  • A 180° RF pulse inverts the magnetization at thermal equilibrium
  • Mz recovers through spin–lattice relaxation for a set delay, the inversion time (TI)
  • A 90° excitation, then a readout (spin echo or gradient echo), takes a snapshot of Mz at that TI
  • Repeat for several TIs, then fit the recovery curve to estimate T1
In a typical IR experiment the magnetization at thermal equilibrium is inverted with a 180° RF pulse. After the longitudinal magnetization recovers through spin-lattice relaxation for a predetermined delay (TI), a 90° excitation pulse is applied, followed by a readout imaging sequence (typically spin-echo or gradient-echo) to create a snapshot of the longitudinal magnetization state at that TI.

Where it comes from

  • First developed for NMR in the 1940s (Hahn 1949; Drain 1949)
  • The first T1 map used saturation recovery: a 90° preparation pulse instead of 180° (Pykett 1978)
Inversion recovery was first developed for NMR in the 1940s (Hahn 1949, Drain 1949), and the first T1 map was acquired using a saturation-recovery technique (90° as a preparation pulse instead of 180°) by Pykett 1978.

Strengths, and the catch

Strengths

  • Large dynamic range of signal change
  • Insensitive to pulse sequence parameter imperfections (Stikov 2015)

The catch

  • Conventional implementations need TR on the order of 2 to 5 T1 (Steen 1994)
  • Whole-organ T1 maps are hard to get in a clinically feasible time

So IR is scarcely used in practice, but it is the reference measurement for developing and comparing T1 methods. Several variations make it practical for some applications (Messroghli 2004; Piechnik 2010).

Despite its proven robustness, inversion recovery is scarcely used in practice because conventional implementations require TRs on the order of 2 to 5 T1, making whole-organ maps challenging in a clinically feasible time. Nonetheless, it is continuously used as a reference measurement during the development of new techniques, or when comparing different T1 mapping techniques.

Signal modelling: the general equation

Steady-state Mz from the Bloch equations for {θ180 – TI – θ90 – (TR − TI)}:

$$M_{z}(TI) = M_0 \frac{1-\cos(\theta_{180})e^{- \frac{TR}{T_1}} -[1-\cos(\theta_{180})]e^{- \frac{TI}{T_1}}}{1 - \cos(\theta_{180}) \cos(\theta_{90}) e^{- \frac{TR}{T_1}}}\qquad\text{(2.1)}$$
  • Mz is the longitudinal magnetization just before the θ90 pulse
  • For the in-phase real signal, multiply by \(k\sin(\theta_{90})\,e^{-TE/T_2}\), where k is a constant
The steady-state longitudinal magnetization of an IR experiment can be derived from the Bloch equations for the pulse sequence θ180 – TI – θ90 – (TR−TI). Mz is the longitudinal magnetization prior to the θ90 pulse. If the in-phase real signal is desired, multiply Eq. 2.1 by k·sin(θ90)·e^(−TE/T2), where k is a constant.

Simplifying: constants and an ideal inversion

Group the constants for each measurement (the same TE and θ90 at every TI) and assume an ideal inversion pulse:

$$M_z(TI) = C\left(1-2e^{- \frac{TI}{T_1}} + e^{- \frac{TR}{T_1}}\right)\qquad\text{(2.2)}$$

C groups the first three terms and the denominator of Eq. 2.1.

The general equation can be simplified by grouping together the constants for each measurement regardless of their values (at each TI, the same TE and θ90 are used) and assuming an ideal inversion pulse. The first three terms and the denominator of Eq. 2.1 are grouped into the constant C.

Simplifying further: the long-TR approximation

If TR is long enough for full relaxation (TR > 5 T1), drop the last term of Eq. 2.2:

$$M_z(TI) = C\left(1-2e^{- \frac{TI}{T_1}}\right)\qquad\text{(2.3)}$$
  • Simple in both equation and experiment: the most widely used IR signal model
  • Many implementations acquire magnitude-only images, so the measured signal is proportional to |Eq. 2.3|
If the experiment is designed such that TR is long enough to allow for full relaxation (TR > 5 T1), the last term of Eq. 2.2 can be dropped. Its simplicity has made Eq. 2.3 the most widely used equation for IR T1 mapping. In many practical implementations only magnitude images are acquired, so the measured signal is proportional to the absolute value of Eq. 2.3.

When the approximation breaks down

In practice, Eq. 2.1 is the better choice for simulating an IR experiment:

  • TR is chosen to exceed 5 T1 of the tissue of interest, which is rarely the longest T1 present: long enough for white matter, but not for CSF
  • Eq. 2.3 also assumes ideal inversion pulses, rarely the case because of slice profile effects

Data fitting: what to watch for

  • Magnitude-only images: polarity inversion restores the exponential form, but it is sensitive to noise: Rician noise gives a non-zero level at the signal null
  • Phase data available: a phase term must be added to the fitting equation (Barral 2010)
  • Eq. 2.3 must only be used for long-TR data (TR > 5 T1), which in practice is rarely true for every tissue
If only magnitude images are available, a polarity inversion is often implemented to restore the non-exponential magnitude curves into the exponential form. This is sensitive to noise due to the Rician noise creating a non-zero level at the signal null. If phase data is available, a phase term must be added to the fitting equation (Barral 2010). Eq. 2.3 must only be used in the long-TR regime, rarely satisfied for all tissues in subjects.

Fitting algorithms

Early methods, built for the computers of the time

  • Null method: each T1 has a unique zero crossing (Pykett 1983)
  • Linear fit of a rearranged Eq. 2.3 on a semi-log plot (Fukushima 1981)

Today

  • Non-linear least squares, e.g. Levenberg–Marquardt, on the approximate (Eq. 2.3) or general (Eq. 2.1) model
Early implementations were designed around the computational power available at the time: the null method (Pykett 1983), assuming each T1 has unique zero-crossings, and linear fitting of a rearranged Eq. 2.3 on a semi-log plot (Fukushima 1981). Nowadays a non-linear least-squares algorithm such as Levenberg-Marquardt is more appropriate, applied to either Eq. 2.3 or Eq. 2.1.

Reduced-dimension NLS (RD-NLS)

  • Fits Eq. 2.1 up to 75× faster than Levenberg–Marquardt, without a precision penalty (Barral 2010)
  • A simplified 5-parameter model is sufficient for accurate T1 mapping:
$$S(TI) = a + b\,e^{- \frac{TI}{T_1}}\qquad\text{(2.4)}$$
  • a and b are complex; for magnitude-only data, a 3-parameter fit of |Eq. 2.4| can be sufficient
  • General: no assumption about TR, so as robust as Eq. 2.1 when every parameter except TI is kept constant
  • Open-source code released with the paper, and available as a qMRLab T1 model
Barral 2010 showed that T1 maps can be fitted up to 75 times faster than Levenberg-Marquardt, without a precision penalty, using reduced-dimension non-linear least squares. Eq. 2.4 is general: no assumption is made about TR. The code was released open source and is available as a qMRLab T1 model. Why it is the gold standard

  • A typical protocol uses a long TR and enough inversion times for a stable fit (typically 5 or more), covering [0, TR]
  • A wide dynamic range, up to [−kM0, kM0]: many TIs with high SNR along the recovery curve (Fukushima 1981)
  • Largely insensitive to flip-angle inaccuracies and imperfect spoiling (Stikov 2015): only TI changes, and a single acquisition is made per TR
The conventional IR experiment is considered the gold standard for several reasons: a long TR and a sufficient number of TIs (typically 5 or more) covering [0, TR]; a wide dynamic range of signals, allowing a number of TIs with high SNR; and T1 maps that are largely insensitive to inaccurate excitation flip angles and imperfect spoiling, since all parameters except TI are constant and only one acquisition is made per TR.

Protocol design

  • Avoid inversion times that null the signal of the tissue of interest: those magnitude images are dominated by Rician noise, which can hurt the fit at low SNR
  • Runs on standard pulse sequences available on most scanners, using a customized protocol
  • Needs no extra calibration measurements

The main drawback: time

  • Long TRs are needed: a few T1 for general models (Eqs. 2.1, 2.4), up to 5 T1 for the long-TR model (Eq. 2.3)
  • One TI per TR (2–5 s) and one phase-encode line per excitation (~100–200 lines): 10–25 minutes for a single-slice T1 map
  • So whole-organ maps are hard to get in clinically feasible times

Still useful as a reference measurement, or for a single-slice T1 estimate used to optimize other pulse sequences.

An important drawback is the need for long TR values. It takes about 10–25 minutes to acquire a single-slice T1 map, as only one TI is acquired per TR (2–5 s) and conventional cartesian readouts acquire one phase-encode line per excitation (~100–200 lines). Nonetheless IR is useful as a reference, or to get T1 estimates of a tissue to optimize other sequences.

Faster variants: Look-Locker

  • After the inversion, a periodic train of small pulses θ samples the recovery (Look 1970):
$$\theta_{180} - \tau - \theta - \tau - \theta - \dots \qquad \tau = TR/n$$
  • Samples the inversion recovery curve much more efficiently, at the cost of lower SNR
  • Each TI depends on the previous θ pulses, so it is more sensitive to B1 inhomogeneity and imperfect spoiling (Gai 2013; Stikov 2015)
  • Widely used for rapid T1 mapping; MOLLI and ShMOLLI for cardiac T1 mapping (Messroghli 2004; Piechnik 2010)
Instead of a single 90° acquisition per TR, Look-Locker applies a periodic train of small excitation pulses after the inversion, with τ = TR/n and n the number of samples. This samples the recovery much more efficiently, but at a cost of lower SNR, and because each TI measurement depends on the previous excitations it is more sensitive to B1 inhomogeneities and imperfect spoiling than IR.

Saturation recovery

  • The inversion pulse is replaced with a saturation pulse: θ90 – TI – θ90; used for the very first T1 map (Pykett 1978)
  • No long TR needed: every θ90 resets Mz to the same initial state
  • But:
    • TIs still need to reach the order of T1
    • The dynamic range is halved: [0, M0]
    • The shortest TIs, the fastest to acquire, have the lowest SNR
Saturation recovery replaces the inversion pulse with a saturation pulse. Unlike IR it does not need a long TR, since every θ90 pulse resets the longitudinal magnetization to the same initial state. However, TIs still need to reach the order of T1 to sample the curve, the dynamic range is cut in half, and the short TIs (fastest acquisitions) have the lowest SNR.

Method 2 · Variable flip angle

Variable Flip Angle T1 Mapping

Also known as DESPOT1: rapid quantitative T1, widely used to acquire 3D (e.g. whole-brain) maps in a clinically feasible time

Variable flip angle (VFA) T1 mapping (Christensen 1974, Fram 1987, Gupta 1977), also known as Driven Equilibrium Single Pulse Observation of T1 (DESPOT1) (Homer 1985, Deoni 2003), is a rapid quantitative T1 measurement technique widely used to acquire 3D T1 maps in a clinically feasible time.

The idea

  • Acquire several spoiled gradient echo images, each with a different excitation flip angle θn (θi ≠ θj)
  • Very short TRs, on the order of 10 ms
  • The steady-state signal is very sensitive to T1 over a wide range of flip angles
VFA estimates T1 values by acquiring multiple spoiled gradient echo acquisitions, each with different excitation flip angles (θn for n = 1, 2, …, N and θi ≠ θj). The steady-state signal of this pulse sequence uses very short TRs (on the order of 10 ms) and is very sensitive to T1 for a wide range of flip angles.

Origins and strengths

  • Originates from NMR: adopted for its time efficiency and its ability to measure accurate T1 values simultaneously over a wide range (Christensen 1974; Gupta 1977)
  • For imaging, VFA can use a 3D acquisition instead of multislice:
    • higher SNR
    • reduced slice profile effects
VFA originates from the NMR field and was adopted because of its time efficiency and its ability to acquire accurate T1 values simultaneously for a wide range of values. For imaging, VFA also benefits from an increase in SNR because it can be acquired using a 3D acquisition instead of multislice, which also helps to reduce slice profile effects.

The catch: flip angle accuracy

  • The signal is very sensitive to inaccuracies in the flip angle
  • The nominal flip angle (set at the scanner) differs from the actual one experienced by the spins: up to ±30 % at 3.0 T, increasing with field strength
  • VFA typically needs another quantitative map, the transmit RF amplitude B1+ (B1), to calibrate the flip angle (Sled 1998)
  • The extra B1 map reduces the time savings, and its errors propagate into the T1 map (Boudreau 2017; Lee 2017)
One important drawback of VFA is that the signal is very sensitive to inaccuracies in the flip angle value. In practice the nominal flip angle differs from the actual flip angle (at 3.0 T, variations of up to ±30%), an issue that increases with field strength. The need for an additional B1 map reduces the time savings offered by VFA over saturation-recovery techniques, and inaccuracies or imprecisions of the B1 map are also propagated into the VFA T1 map.

The Ernst angle

The flip angle that maximizes the steady-state signal depends on T1 and TR. It can be solved analytically from Eq. 2.5 (Ernst 1966):

$$\theta_{Ernst} = \text{acos}\left(e^{- \frac{TR}{T_1}}\right)\qquad\text{(2.6)}$$
From Figure 2.8 it is clearly seen that the flip angle at which the steady-state signal is maximized depends on the T1 and TR values. This flip angle is the Ernst angle (Ernst 1966), which can be solved analytically from Eq. 2.5 using calculus.

Model assumptions

The closed-form solution (Eq. 2.5) assumes:

  • The longitudinal magnetization has reached a steady state after a large number of TRs
  • The transverse magnetization is perfectly spoiled at the end of each TR

These may not hold if care is not taken. Bloch simulations, which solve the Bloch equations numerically for a set of spins at each time point, give a more realistic estimate when a steady state is not reached.

The closed-form solution makes several assumptions which in practice may not always hold if care is not taken: that the longitudinal magnetization has reached a steady state after a large number of TRs, and that the transverse magnetization is perfectly spoiled at the end of each TR. Bloch simulations provide a more realistic estimate of the signal if the number of repetitions is small.

Spoiling

  • Sufficient spoiling is likely the most challenging parameter to control in a VFA experiment
  • A combination of gradient spoiling and RF phase spoiling is typically recommended (Handbook 2004; Zur 1991)
  • RF phase increment after each TR: \(\Phi_{n}=\Phi_{n-1}+n\Phi_{0}\), with \(\Phi_0 = 117°\) (Zur 1991)
  • Very strong gradients introduce diffusion effects that further improve spoiling (Yarnykh 2010)

Data fitting: linearizing the signal

  • Non-linear least squares on Eq. 2.5 (two free variables: T1 and M0) is valid, but rarely done in practice
  • A simple refactoring of Eq. 2.5, without any approximation, gives the form y = mx + b (Gupta 1977):
$$\frac{S_n}{\sin(\theta_n)} = e^{- \frac{TR}{T_1}} \frac{S_n}{\tan(\theta_n)} + C \left(1-e^{- \frac{TR}{T_1}}\right)\qquad\text{(2.7)}$$

The last term is the same for every θn, so it can be grouped into the constant:

$$\frac{S_n}{\sin(\theta_n)} = e^{- \frac{TR}{T_1}} \frac{S_n}{\tan(\theta_n)} + C\qquad\text{(2.8)}$$
One could fit VFA data with a non-linear least squares algorithm such as Levenberg-Marquardt using Eq. 2.5, typically with two free variables (T1 and M0). This is rarely done in practice because a simple refactoring of Eq. 2.5 allows T1 to be estimated with a linear least squares algorithm, which substantially reduces processing time.

T1 from the slope

A linear regression of Sn/sin(θn) against Sn/tan(θn) gives T1 from its slope:

$$T_1 = - \frac{TR}{\ln(slope)}\qquad\text{(2.9)}$$
  • With only two flip angles, a very common protocol, the slope is simply the elementary slope between the two points

Next: both forms of the signal (Eq. 2.5 and Eq. 2.8) for a noisy dataset.

With the rearranged form of Eq. 2.5, T1 can be simply estimated from the slope of a linear regression of Sn/sin(θn) and Sn/tan(θn). If data were acquired with only two flip angles, the slope can be calculated using the elementary slope equation.

Protocol design: which flip angles?

  • Most VFA experiments use the minimum number of flip angles, two, to minimize acquisition time
  • For a single T1, the best precision comes from the two flip angles that give 71 % of the maximum signal (at the Ernst angle) (Deoni 2003; Schabel 2008)
  • Time allowing, extra flip angles are acquired at higher values and in between, where differences between tissue T1 values are larger
  • With more than two angles, the points no longer share the same noise weighting, which may bias linear fits at low SNR: use non-linear least squares or a weighted linear fit (Chang 2008)
There are two important protocol design considerations: how many and which flip angles to use, and correcting inaccurate flip angles due to transmit RF field inhomogeneity. For more than two flip angles, Eqs. 2.5 and 2.8 do not have the same noise weighting for each fitting point, which may bias linear least-squares T1 estimates at lower SNRs; low SNR data should be fitted with Eq. 2.5 using non-linear least squares, or with a weighted linear least-squares form of Eq. 2.8.

Correcting the flip angle with a B1 map

  • The excitation RF field (B1+) of a loaded coil varies in space, unless RF shimming is available, which is not common at clinical field strengths (Sled 1998)
  • Correct the flip angle voxelwise with a B1 map acquired in the same session:
$$\theta_{corrected} = B_1\, \theta_{nominal}\qquad\text{(2.10)}$$
  • B1 is normalized: unitless, and equal to 1 where the RF field has the expected amplitude

Next: fitted T1 from Monte Carlo data simulated with biased flip angles, fitted without and with B1 correction.

Accurate knowledge of the flip angle values is very important for accurate T1 maps. Because of how the RF field interacts with matter, the excitation field of a loaded RF coil varies spatially. For methods sensitive to this, like VFA, the flip angle can be corrected voxelwise by multiplying the nominal value by a scaling factor B1 from a B1 map acquired in the same session.

Pitfalls

  • Accuracy is very sensitive to the pulse sequence implementation, so VFA is less robust than inversion recovery (Baudrexel 2017; Lutti 2013; Stikov 2015)
  • Insufficient spoiling can bias T1 by up to 30 % relative to inversion recovery (Stikov 2015)
  • Accuracy and precision depend strongly on the quality of the B1 map (Lee 2017), which varies between implementations (Boudreau 2017)
  • Omitting B1 correction is not recommended: most scanners have default sequences that, with careful settings, give B1 maps of sufficient quality very rapidly (Boudreau 2017; Samson 2006; Wang 2005)
Modern rapid B1 mapping sequences are not as widely available as VFA, so some groups generate an artificial B1 map with image processing (Liberman 2013) or omit B1 correction altogether (Yuan 2012). The latter is not recommended, because most scanners have default sequences that can provide B1 maps of sufficient quality very rapidly.

Why VFA is still widely used

  • One of the most widely used T1 mapping methods in research
  • Rapid acquisition and rapid image processing
  • Widespread availability
  • A great candidate for other quantitative protocols: quantitative MT (Cercignani 2005; Yarnykh 2002) and DCE (Li 2018; Sung 2013)
Despite some drawbacks, VFA is still one of the most widely used T1 mapping methods in research. Its rapid acquisition time, rapid image processing time, and widespread availability make it a great candidate for use in other quantitative imaging protocols like qMT and DCE.

Method 3 · Dictionary-based

MP2RAGE

Magnetization Prepared 2 Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echoes

MP2RAGE: Magnetization Prepared 2 Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echoes (Marques 2010).

Dictionary-based techniques

  • Growing in popularity: available on clinical scanners, rapid scan times, fast post-processing
  • They use numerical dictionaries: databases of pre-calculated signal values simulated for a wide range of tissue and protocol combinations
  • Examples for T1: MR Fingerprinting (Ma 2013), some compressed sensing methods (Doneva 2010; Li 2012), and MP2RAGE (Marques 2010)
  • Two categories:
    • dictionaries that exploit redundancy in parametric data to accelerate imaging (CS, MRF)
    • dictionaries that estimate quantitative maps from the reconstructed images (MP2RAGE)
Dictionary-based techniques are increasing in popularity due to their growing availability on clinical scanners, rapid scan times and fast post-processing, making quantitative T1 mapping accessible for clinical applications. Because MP2RAGE is implemented primarily for T1 mapping and is increasingly available as a standard sequence, the chapter focuses on it; many concepts are shared by other dictionary-based techniques.

MP2RAGE: a hybrid of IR and VFA

  • An extension of the conventional MPRAGE sequence widely used in clinical studies (Haase 1989; Mugler 1990)
  • A 180° inversion pulse prepares the magnetization for T1 sensitivity at the start of each TRMP2RAGE
  • Then two images at two inversion times, using gradient echo (GRE) blocks with low flip angles and short TRs
MP2RAGE can be seen as a hybrid between the inversion recovery and VFA pulse sequences: a 180° inversion pulse prepares the magnetization for T1 sensitivity at the beginning of each TR_MP2RAGE, and two images are then acquired at different inversion times using gradient recalled echo imaging blocks with low flip angles and short repetition times.

Encoding, and why two images

  • Within a GRE block, each excitation is followed by a constant in-plane ("y") phase encode, varied for each TRMP2RAGE, and a different 3D ("z") phase encode, varied at each TR
  • The centre of k-space in the 3D direction is acquired at each block's TI
  • Motivation: an MPRAGE-like image with self-bias correction of B0 and B1−, and a first-order correction of B1+
  • Because two TIs are acquired (MPRAGE acquires one), T1 can be inferred, giving quantitative T1 maps
The main motivation for developing MP2RAGE was to provide a metric similar to MPRAGE but with self-bias correction of the static (B0) and receive (B1−) fields, and a first-order correction of the transmit field (B1+). Because two images at different TIs are acquired, information about T1 can also be inferred.

Figure 2.13: simplified diagram of an MP2RAGE pulse sequence. The readouts in each TR use a constant in-plane phase encode ("y") gradient set for each TR_MP2RAGE, but varying 3D phase encode ("z") gradients between successive TRs.

The MP2RAGE signal

For complex data (magnitude and phase, or real and imaginary), SMP2RAGE combines the images at the two TIs (Marques 2010):

$$S_{\text{MP2RAGE}}=\text{real}\left( \frac{S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{1}}}^{\ast}S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{2}}}^{\ast}}{\left| S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{1}}} \right|^{2}+ \left| S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{2}}} \right|^{2}} \right)\qquad\text{(2.11)}$$
  • Bounded between [−0.5, 0.5]
  • The phase data help reduce some B0 inhomogeneity effects
The MP2RAGE parameter is calculated in addition to the T1 map. For complex data it is computed from the images at the two TIs with Eq. 2.11. It is bounded between −0.5 and 0.5, and helps reduce some B0 inhomogeneity effects using the phase data. (Eq. 2.11 is shown as printed in the book.)

Real or polarity-restored data

For real data, or magnitude data with polarity restoration:

$$S_{\text{MP2RAGE}}=\text{real}\left( \frac{S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{1}}}^{\ast}S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{2}}}^{\ast}}{S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{1}}}^{2}+ S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{2}}}^{2}} \right)\qquad\text{(2.12)}$$
For real data, or magnitude data with polarity restoration, the MP2RAGE metric is instead calculated with Eq. 2.12.

Signal equations: assumptions

  • MP2RAGE is a hybrid of IR and VFA, so its signal equations are more complex
  • A steady state is not reached during the short GRE train: the signal at the centre of k-space, which defines the contrast, depends on the number of phase-encoding steps
  • The equations that follow assume the 3D phase-encoding dimension is fully sampled: no partial Fourier or parallel imaging

Derivation: appendix of Marques et al. 2010.

Typically a steady state is not achieved during the short train of GRE imaging blocks, so the signal at the centre of k-space for each readout depends on the number of phase-encoding steps. For simplicity, the equations assume the 3D phase-encoding dimension is fully sampled (no partial Fourier or parallel imaging acceleration).

Signal at the two inversion times

$$\begin{aligned} S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{1}}}=&B_{1}^{-}e^{-\text{TE}/T_{2}^{\ast }}M_{0}\sin\left( \theta_{1} \right) \\ &\times \Bigg[ \left( \frac{-\text{eff}\,m_{z,ss}}{M_{0}}\text{EA}+\left( 1-\text{EA} \right) \right)\left( \cos\left( \theta_{1} \right) \text{ER} \right)^{n/2-1}\\ &\quad+\left( 1-\text{ER} \right) \frac{1-\left( \cos\left( \theta_{1} \right)\text{ER} \right)^{n/2-1}}{1-\cos\left( \theta_{1} \right)\text{ER}} \Bigg] \end{aligned}\qquad\text{(2.13)}$$
$$\begin{aligned} S_{\text{GRE}_{\text{TI}_{2}}}=&B_{1}^{-}e^{-\text{TE}/T_{2}^{\ast }}M_{0}\sin\left( \theta_{2} \right) \\ &\times \Bigg[\frac{ \frac{m_{z,ss}}{M_{0}}\text{EA}+\left( 1-\text{EC} \right)}{\text{EC}\left( \cos\left( \theta_{2} \right)\text{ER} \right)^{n/2}}-\left( 1-\text{ER} \right)\frac{\left( \cos\left( \theta_{2} \right)\text{ER} \right)^{-n/2}-1 }{1-\cos\left( \theta_{2} \right)\text{ER} } \Bigg] \end{aligned}\qquad\text{(2.14)}$$
Eqs. 2.13 and 2.14: GRE signals at the two inversion times, for a fully sampled 3D phase-encode direction (see Marques 2010, appendix, for the derivation). The definitions of the terms are on the next slide.

Definitions

  • B1−: receive field sensitivity
  • eff: adiabatic inversion pulse efficiency
  • ER = exp(−TR/T1), EA = exp(−TA/T1), EB = exp(−TB/T1), EC = exp(−TC/T1)
  • n: number of voxels in the 3D phase-encode direction, varied within each GRE block
  • mz,ss: steady-state Mz just before the inversion pulse
  • TA: inversion pulse → start of GRE1; TA = TI1 − (n/2)TR
  • TB: end of GRE1 → start of GRE2; TB = TI2 − (TI1 + nTR)
  • TC: end of GRE2 → end of the TRMP2RAGE; TC = TRMP2RAGE − (TI2 + (n/2)TR)
  • (TA, TB, TC as given for no k-space acceleration)
TA: time between the inversion pulse and the beginning of the GRE1 block; TB: time between the end of GRE1 and the beginning of GRE2; TC: time between the end of GRE2 and the end of the TR. The expressions for TA, TB and TC hold when there is no partial Fourier or parallel imaging acceleration. Note: the book writes TC with TI1; since TC runs from the end of GRE2, it is TI2 here.

Steady state before the inversion

$$m_{z,ss}=\frac{M_{0}\left[ \beta\left( \cos\left( \theta_{2} \right)\text{ER} \right)^{n}+\left( 1-\text{ER} \right)\frac{1-\left( \cos\left( \theta_{2} \right)\text{ER} \right)^{n}}{1-\cos\left( \theta_{2} \right)\text{ER} } \right]\text{EC}+\left( 1- \text{EC}\right)}{1+\text{eff}\left( \cos\left( \theta_{1} \right) \cos\left( \theta_{2} \right) \right)^{n}e^{-TR_{\text{MP2RAGE}}/T_{1}}}\qquad\text{(2.15)}$$
$$\beta=\bigg( \left( 1-\text{EA} \right) \left( \cos\left( \theta_{1}\right)\text{ER} \right)^{n}+\left( 1-\text{ER} \right)\frac{1-\left( \cos\left( \theta_{1}\right)\text{ER} \right)^{n}}{1-\cos\left( \theta_{1}\right)\text{ER} }\bigg)\text{EB}+\left( 1-\text{EB} \right)\qquad\text{(2.16)}$$
mz,ss is the steady-state longitudinal magnetization prior to the inversion pulse (Eqs. 2.15 and 2.16). Note: the "=" after m_z,ss is missing from Eq. 2.15 in the book; it is added here.

What SMP2RAGE removes, and what remains

Cancels out

  • Receive field sensitivity, B1−
  • T2*
  • M0

Remains

  • Transmit field B1+, hidden in θ1 and θ2
  • Can be reduced by careful protocol design (Marques 2010), but not entirely eliminated (Marques 2013)
From Eqs. 2.13–2.16, the MP2RAGE parameter (Eqs. 2.11, 2.12) cancels out the effects of receive field sensitivity, T2* and M0. The sensitivity to the transmit field, hidden within θ1 and θ2, can be reduced by careful protocol design but not entirely eliminated.

Data fitting with a dictionary

  • No conventional minimization (e.g. Levenberg–Marquardt): MP2RAGE uses pre-calculated signal values over a wide range of T1, then interpolates to find the T1 that matches the observed signal
  • Dictionaries can be generated before scanning, and interpolating is much faster than most fitting algorithms, so maps can be shown directly on the scanner console
  • Signal curves must be unique for each parameter value: SMP2RAGE is unique for each T1 for a given protocol, so a [T1, SMP2RAGE] dictionary is enough (Marques 2010)
  • B1 sensitivity is reduced with protocol optimization, and further by adding B1 as a dictionary dimension [T1, B1, SMP2RAGE], at the cost of an extra B1 map (Marques 2013)
Dictionary-based techniques do not typically use conventional minimization algorithms. Instead they use pre-calculated signal values for a wide range of parameter values and interpolate within the dictionary to estimate the T1 that matches the observed signal. Adding B1 as a dictionary dimension requires an additional B1 map, which lengthens the scan time.

Software

  • MP2RAGE is increasingly distributed by MRI vendors, typically with a fitting package that reconstructs T1 maps online
  • Several open-source packages are also available (Marques 2017; de Hollander 2017)
  • For new users these are recommended over programming one from scratch: there are many potential pitfalls, e.g. adjusting the equations for partial Fourier or parallel imaging
The MP2RAGE pulse sequence is increasingly distributed by MRI vendors, typically with a data fitting package to reconstruct T1 maps online. Open source packages are recommended for new users over programming one from scratch, as there are many potential pitfalls.

Benefits

  • Widespread availability and turnkey acquisition and fitting: a main reason for growing interest in quantitative T1 in clinical and neuroscience studies
  • Highly reproducible brain T1 in an inter- and intra-site study at eight sites (same hardware and software, 7T) (Voelker 2016)
  • Among the fastest acquisition and post-processing times of quantitative T1 methods
  • Very high resolution: 1 mm isotropic at 3T and submillimeter at 7T, both in under 10 min (Fujimoto 2014), opening the door to cortical studies (Waehnert 2014; Beck 2018; Haast 2018)
This widespread availability and turnkey acquisition/fitting procedures are a main contributing factor to the growing interest in quantitative T1 maps in clinical and neuroscience studies. MP2RAGE has one of the fastest acquisition and post-processing times, while acquiring very high resolution T1 maps.

Pitfalls

  • Reproducibility depends on using one pre-calculated dictionary: different dictionaries (e.g. across sites and vendors) give minor differences in T1 for the same data
  • Residual B1 sensitivity can be large enough to need correction with a measured B1 map (Marques 2013; Haast 2018), but B1 mapping brings its own errors: choose the method and post-processing (e.g. filtering) carefully (Boudreau 2017)
  • The equations, and so the dictionaries, assume monoexponential longitudinal relaxation: suboptimal estimates of the long T1 component under a biexponential model (Rioux 2016), more important at higher fields
MP2RAGE and similar dictionary-based techniques have limitations to consider before incorporating them in a study: dictionary dependence, residual B1 sensitivity (with B1 mapping as an additional source of error), and the monoexponential relaxation assumption.

Summary

Inversion recovery

Gold standard: wide dynamic range, insensitive to flip angle and spoiling errors.

Long TR: 10–25 min per slice. Used as a reference.

Variable flip angle

Rapid 3D acquisition and processing; widely available.

Sensitive to spoiling (up to 30 % bias) and needs a good B1 map.

MP2RAGE

Turnkey, fast, high resolution and reproducible.

Depends on its dictionary; residual B1+ sensitivity.
