Ask the class to hover over the green MW curve and type the readings. Three clicks. 1: the three levels: 50% drop leaves 0.50 of the signal, 63% leaves 0.37, 90% leaves 0.10. 2: where the MW curve crosses each level, read from the book's own curve: about 13, 19 and 44 ms. 3: why: on a mono-exponential, each drop happens at a fixed multiple of T2: ln 2 ≈ 0.69 T2 for 50%, about 1 T2 for 63% (1 − 1/e = 63.2%), ln 10 ≈ 2.3 T2 for 90%. d. A mono-exponential fit of a 50/50 voxel

d. Determine the (erroneous) mono-exponential T2 value for voxels that would contain 50% MW and 50% IEW. Is it closer to the T2 value of MW or IEW?

  • T2 ≈ 78 ms, closer to IEW (121 ms) than to MW (19 ms).
  • MW is mostly gone after about 40 ms: most echoes see only IEW.
  • It depends on the echoes: TE ≥ 50 ms only gives 115 ms. Not a tissue property.
The slide opens on the book figure. Three clicks. 1: sets the dotted curve to exactly 50% MW and 50% IEW, from the book's own MW and IEW curves (the figure first shows the simulation's fitted mix, 52% MW, although its slider says 50%). 2: a least-squares fit of Eq. 3.2, S0 e^(−TE/T2), with S0 and T2 free, on the figure's 100 echoes from 0 to 300 ms: T2 ≈ 78 ms and S0 ≈ 0.81. The fit misses the fast drop at the start, which is why S0 is below 1. 3: the answer: closer to IEW. And it is not a property of the tissue: with echoes only from 50 ms on, the same voxel gives about 115 ms, almost the IEW value. This is why multi-exponential fitting is needed for MWF.
