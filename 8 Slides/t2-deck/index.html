T2 and T2*

T2: spin–spin relaxation

Spins dephase through interactions with their neighbours, so the transverse magnetization decays.

T2*: T2 + field inhomogeneities

Spins also precess at slightly different speeds, fan out, and the signal decays faster.

  • Macroscopic: metallic implants, air–tissue interfaces
  • Microscopic: susceptibility differences between tissues

(Chavhan 2009; Cohen-Adad 2014)

T2 relaxation, also known as the transverse or spin-spin relaxation, is characterized by the dephasing of spins, leading to a reduction of the total magnetization in the x-y plane. The T2 value represents the upper limit of the signal decay time under ideal imaging conditions. In practice, magnetic field inhomogeneities, macroscopic (metallic implants, air-tissue interfaces) or microscopic (susceptibility differences between tissues), cause the transverse magnetization to decay faster. When considering this phenomenon, we refer to the transverse relaxation as T2*. The animation: after a 90° pulse, M (in the rotating frame, so without the precession) shrinks with T2 = 100 ms; the signal |Mxy| decays as e^(−t/T2). Tick “Field inhomogeneities” and play again: M is now a fan of spins at slightly different frequencies (red faster, blue slower; a Lorentzian spread with T2′ = 30 ms), which spread out while each still shrinks with T2, so their sum decays faster, with T2* = 23 ms (1/T2* = 1/T2 + 1/T2′, Eq. 3.3); the T2 curve stays dashed for comparison. Exercise 2-1 takes this fan further (what a 180° does to it).

Why map T2?

  • A T2 map gives T2 in every voxel: a window on tissue composition
  • Sensitive to iron, myelination, inflammation (Dortch 2020)
  • Newer methods, e.g. MR fingerprinting, map several parameters at once (Ma 2013; Hamilton 2017)

Hover over the images to read their values. Data: the book’s Figure 3.1. T2 colour map: log-processed Navia, as recommended for relaxometry maps (Fuderer 2025).

T2 mapping offers images of T2 relaxation times (T2 maps) providing valuable insights into tissue composition. Given that T2 relaxation is sensitive to specific microstructural changes associated with diseases, such as iron accumulation, myelination and inflammation (Dortch 2020), it is considered a promising modality for clinical and research applications. Novel techniques have emerged beyond the conventional ones developed for NMR, such as MR fingerprinting (Ma 2013), a fast relaxation mapping technique that uses a single image acquisition to quantify multiple parameters (Hamilton 2017). The images: the book’s Figure 3.1 data, its T2-weighted image at TE = 12.8 ms beside its T2 map (hover for values). The T2w image is qualitative: its grey levels are a contrast, in arbitrary units that depend on the scanner, the coil and the settings, read by eye; that is what makes it useful for diagnosis. The T2 map is quantitative: each voxel holds a measurement of one physical quantity, T2, in ms, which can be compared between scans, sites and patients. The T2 map uses the colour map recommended for T2 maps by the ISMRM Quantitative MR Study Group (Fuderer et al., Magn Reson Med 2025, doi:10.1002/mrm.30290): Crameri’s Navia, log-processed (equal ratios of T2 look equally different), from 80 to 140 ms, with black for invalid values. The paper makes no recommendation on the range; this one brackets brain tissue (about 89 to 118 ms in this map), so white matter shows dark teal and grey matter green, and fluid saturates to cream. A wide range such as 50 to 200 ms leaves the two tissues in nearly the same shade, since they differ by only about 17%.

What this talk covers

Mono-exponential model

One T2 per voxel: a single compartment.

Multi-exponential model

Several compartments per voxel, each with its own T2; e.g. myelin water fraction.

T2*

How its decay differs from T2; related: QSM.

Commonly used T2-mapping techniques are split into two categories: mono-exponential methods, which consider that the voxel's signal arises from a single tissue compartment, and multi-exponential methods, where various tissues that contribute to a single voxel's signal are considered. The talk (and the chapter) covers signal modeling and data fitting for both, their benefits and pitfalls, an overview of applications (e.g. MWF imaging), and the fundamentals of T2* mapping and how its signal decay curve can differ from that of T2 relaxation.

Method 1 · Mono-exponential

Mono-exponential T2 Mapping

A single fitted T2 value for each voxel

Mono-exponential T2 mapping fits one decay curve, and one T2 value, per voxel.

Acquiring a decay curve

  • Single-echo spin echo: one echo per TR, so a whole new image for each TE, then fit T2 (Milford 2015)
    • Too slow in practice
  • Multi-echo spin echo: several echoes per TR, one every echo spacing (ESP), so every TE in one acquisition (Fatemi 2020; Milford 2015)

The diagrams: the same T2 decay against time, sampled at one echo per acquisition, TE after the excitation (single echo: three acquisitions for three points), or at an echo every echo spacing (ESP) within each TR (multi-echo: one acquisition; echo n is at TE = n × ESP). The simplest technique for T2 mapping is the acquisition of multiple images using a single-echo sequence with different TEs, then fitting T2 from the decaying signal (Milford 2015). It is typically not employed in vivo due to prohibitively long acquisition times. Multi-spin echo sequences, based on the CPMG pulse train, employ multiple 180° refocusing pulses to generate multiple echoes within a single acquisition, substantially reducing scan time and making MSE the sequence of choice in clinical settings.

Carr–Purcell, and the Meiboom–Gill improvement

Carr–Purcell (CP)

  • 90° about x, then 180°s about the same axis (x)
  • Pulse errors accumulate*: the signal drops too fast, so T2 is off (Brown 2014)

Meiboom–Gill (CPMG)

  • 180°s about the perpendicular axis (y)
  • Most errors do not accumulate: a stable echo train (Brown 2014)

* Try it in Exercise 2-3.

The original Carr-Purcell method applies a 90-degree pulse followed by a series of 180-degree pulses along the same axis, designed to mitigate diffusion effects by preventing phase accumulation. However, imperfections in the 180-degree pulses accumulate, resulting in a faster than expected loss of transverse magnetization and inaccurate T2 measurements (Brown 2014). The Meiboom-Gill improvement applies the initial 90-degree pulse along one axis and the subsequent 180-degree pulses along a perpendicular axis; this phase cycling distributes errors such that they average out over time.

The mono-exponential assumption

  • One T2 per voxel rests on two assumptions:
    • the tissue itself has one T2 component (e.g. white matter as a single water pool)
    • the voxel holds only one tissue (e.g. a white matter voxel with no grey matter or CSF in it too)
  • Simple and robust: the most widely used T2 map
  • Often too simple: both can fail, e.g. myelin water within white matter, or CSF at a tissue boundary (Graham 1996)

Multi-exponential models come later in the talk.

To fit the data using the mono-exponential model, homogeneity within each voxel is assumed implicitly, implying a consistent T2 decay time across the tissue. This results in a single fitted T2 value for each voxel. Although widely used, the mono-exponential model has been shown to be insufficient for proper estimation of tissue T2, given that the signal in a voxel often arises from multiple tissue components with different T2 values (Graham 1996).

Signal modelling

The transverse magnetization decays exponentially (Bloch equations):

$$M_{xy}(TE) = M_z(0^{-})\,e^{-TE/T_{2}}\qquad\text{(3.1)}$$
  • Mz(0−) is the longitudinal magnetization immediately before the 90° excitation pulse
  • Assumes the measured signal is proportional to Mxy, and that Mz(0−) is constant regardless of TE (Dortch 2020)

For fitting, the signal, with S0 the signal just after the excitation (Dortch 2020; Milford 2015):

$$S(TE) = S_{0}\,e^{-TE/T_{2}}\qquad\text{(3.2)}$$

The same single-compartment model describes T2* decay: replace T2 by T2*.

The decay of the transverse magnetization is exponential and can be derived from the transverse component of the Bloch equations. Mz(0-) is the longitudinal magnetization immediately preceding the 90 degree excitation pulse. Using this equation assumes that the measured signal is proportional to Mxy, and that Mz(0-) remains constant regardless of echo time (Dortch 2020). Next figure: T2 and T2* decay curves for white and gray matter, with relaxation times from Siemonsen 2008. The T2 signal decay for the mono-exponential model is S(TE) = S0 exp(−TE/T2), where S0 is the signal intensity immediately following the excitation pulse (Dortch 2020; Milford 2015).

Stimulated echoes

  • B1 inhomogeneities and RF pulse imperfections make refocusing pulses deviate from the ideal 180°
  • This generates additional echoes, known as stimulated or spurious echoes
  • They contaminate the signal decay: erroneous T2 estimates (McPhee 2018)

Fitting with improved accuracy

  • Use only the even-numbered echoes (Focke 2011; Kim 2009)
  • Or discard the first echo (Biasiolli 2013; Milford 2015)

Refocusing pulses deviating from 180 degrees generate stimulated or spurious echoes that contaminate the signal decay and result in erroneous T2 estimations (McPhee 2018). Some studies have shown that fitting accuracy can be improved either by using only even-numbered echoes (Focke 2011; Kim 2009), or by discarding the first echo (Biasiolli 2013; Milford 2015).

T2*: faster than T2

  • So far, Mxy decays exponentially with TE / T2 (Eq. 3.1)
  • In practice, B0 inhomogeneities cause a faster loss of the transverse signal: T2* relaxation (Figure 3.3)
$$\frac{1}{T_{2}^{*}} = \frac{1}{T_{2}} + \frac{1}{T_{2}'}\qquad\text{(3.3)}$$
  • T2′ quantifies the part of the relaxation that is due to magnetic field inhomogeneities (Brown 2014)
Until now, we have assumed that Mxy decays exponentially with TE divided by T2. In practice, other factors such as B0 inhomogeneities cause a more rapid loss of the transverse signal, a faster decay referred to as T2* relaxation. T2' quantifies the portion of relaxation which is due to magnetic field inhomogeneities. (The chapter's text here points to Eq. 3.3 and Figure 3.1; the T2 model is Eq. 3.1 and the T2* curves are Figure 3.3.)

T2′ and measuring T2*

  • T2′ maps (T2* with the T2 part removed): blood oxygenation, infarct growth in stroke (Lee 2014; Siemonsen 2008)
  • T2* is fitted like T2, from gradient echoes, which do not refocus field inhomogeneities (Cohen-Adad 2014)
$$S(TE) = S_{0}\,e^{-TE/T_{2}^{*}}\qquad\text{(3.4)}$$
  • SAGE (spin and gradient echo): T2 and T2* in one acquisition (Stokes 2014)
Some studies suggest that T2' mapping could offer valuable insights for brain disease diagnosis, notably by quantifying blood oxygenation levels (Lee 2014) and predicting infarct growth in acute stroke patients (Siemonsen 2008). The T2* decay can be fitted using the same methods as T2. Unlike T2 mapping, which uses spin echo type sequences, T2* mapping is performed with gradient echo sequences, as they are sensitive to magnetic field inhomogeneities. Specialized sequences such as SAGE enable simultaneous acquisition of both T2 and T2*. Note: the book's Eq. 3.4 shows T2 in the exponent; the slide uses T2*, as the text describes.

Noise and the T2 fit

  • Noise makes the fit less accurate, worst pixel by pixel (lower SNR than averaging over a region) (Sandino 2015)
  • Magnitude images have a noise floor: late echoes level off above zero, so T2 comes out too long unless the floor is modelled

Use the menu to change the SNR; the fitted T2 is in the legend.

In MRI, noise in the data can make it harder to accurately fit the T2 decay curve, which is problematic given the necessity for highly precise T2 values in clinical contexts. This is particularly pronounced with pixel-wise T2 mapping, as the SNR is much lower than in ROI T2 mapping approaches (Sandino 2015). On the noise floor: a magnitude image cannot go below zero, so once the signal sinks to the level of the noise it levels off (a Rician floor) instead of decaying; fitted with a plain exponential, those late echoes pull T2 long, unless the floor is modelled or the late echoes are left out. Figure 3.4: impact of noise on T2 relaxometry fitting, for a single voxel with a true T2 of 109 ms. As the noise level increases, the fitting accuracy decreases and the estimated T2 deviates from the true value (about 101 ms at SNR 10, 109 ms at SNR 130). A low SNR can have a considerable impact on the fit. Exercise 4 uses this simulation.

How many echoes, and when?

  • A trade-off: echo spacing, heating (SAR), and low SNR at late echoes (Shrager 1998)
  • The Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB) gives the best number of echoes for a given noise level (Cavassila 2001; Jones 1996)
  • Optimized echo times, for the expected range of T2, beat uniform spacing (Shrager 1998)
The number of echoes is influenced by the need for adequate spacing between echoes, the potential risk of heating the sample, and the challenges of processing low-SNR data (Shrager 1998). The CRLB estimates the smallest possible variance of an unbiased estimator given the noise in the data (Cavassila 2001); from it, the optimal number of echoes can be determined (Jones 1996). Shrager 1998 introduced a method that optimizes the selection of echo times based on a predetermined range of expected T2 values, with superior accuracy to uniformly spaced echo times.

Benefits and pitfalls

Benefits

  • Simple to implement and fit
  • Fast with multi-echo spin echo (Fatemi 2020; Milford 2015)

Pitfalls

  • One T2 per voxel over-simplifies complex tissue
  • B1 errors overestimate T2 (Fatemi 2020)

The main benefit of mono-exponential T2 mapping is its simplicity and straightforward implementation; MESE sequences significantly reduce acquisition time. Drawbacks: assuming a single T2 per voxel tends to over-simplify the tissue microstructure, which is particularly problematic for tissues with complex microstructure. MESE sequences are also sensitive to RF pulse imperfections: B1 inhomogeneities and reduced flip angles have been shown to overestimate T2 with mono-exponential methods (Fatemi 2020).

Method 2 · Multi-exponential

Multi-exponential T2 Mapping

The T2 contribution of each tissue compartment within a voxel

Multi-exponential T2 mapping considers the T2 relaxation contribution of each tissue compartment within a voxel.

One voxel, several compartments

  • Multi-exponential models relax both mono-exponential assumptions:
    • several T2 components in one tissue (e.g. white matter: myelin water and intra/extracellular water)
    • several tissues in one voxel (e.g. a white matter voxel with CSF in it too: partial volume)
  • Each compartment gets its own T2 and amplitude
  • Key application: myelin water fraction (MWF) imaging
The mono-exponential curve uses a single compartment tissue model: all tissue components in a voxel are assumed to have the same T2. In practice this can result in inaccurate fittings, as a voxel may contain different tissue compartments with different T2 times, e.g. a voxel at a tissue boundary containing both CSF and gray matter (partial volume effect). Multi-exponential T2 mapping considers the T2 contribution of each compartment within a voxel, which is useful in applications such as myelin water fraction imaging.

Signal model: a sum of exponentials

Each compartment i decays with its own T2,i, from its own amplitude Si (Collewet 2022; Dortch 2020):

$$S(TE) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} S_{i}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,i}}\qquad\text{(3.6)}$$

Two compartments, myelin water (MW) and intra/extracellular water (IEW):

$$S(TE) = S_{MW}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,MW}} + S_{IEW}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,IEW}}\qquad\text{(3.7)}$$
For multi-exponential T2 mapping, the transverse magnetization acquired at different TEs is modelled as a sum of exponential decays, where each term represents the contribution of the ith tissue component (Collewet 2022; Dortch 2020). Using a multi-exponential model rather than mono-exponential for complex tissues like myelin enables more precise quantification of T2 within each voxel. To fit the data, Eq. 3.5 is rewritten in terms of the initial amplitude Si of each tissue component, with T2,i the T2 of that component. The multi-exponential signal of Figure 3.5 is the combination of the myelin water and intra/extracellular water signals (Eq. 3.7). (Eq. 3.5, the same model in terms of M_xy, is in the book.)

From a decay to a T2 distribution

  • In practice: fit many T2 values at once (non-negative least squares), not just two
  • The result is a T2 distribution: in white matter, a short MW peak and a long IEW peak
  • MWF = the area of the MW peak / the area of both*

* Try it in Exercise 2-4.

How MWF is measured in practice: rather than fitting exactly two exponentials (Eq. 3.7), the decay is fitted with many T2 values at once, usually by non-negative least squares (MacKay 1994), which gives a T2 distribution, the amount of signal at each T2. In white matter it has two peaks, myelin water (short T2, about 10 to 40 ms at 3 T) and intra/extracellular water (about 45 to 160 ms); MWF is the area of the MW peak over the area of both, i.e. Eq. 3.8 at TE = 0. The diagram is schematic (MW peak at 20 ms, IEW at 80 ms). Application: myelin water fraction (MWF)

  • MWF measures the water trapped between the myelin sheaths, not myelin itself (Alonso-Ortiz 2015)
  • Myelin water has a short T2, about 20 ms at 3 T: measurable with spin echoes (Lee 2021)
$$MWF = \frac{S_{MW}}{S_{MW} + S_{IEW}}\qquad\text{(3.8)}$$
  • A candidate biomarker in MS: T2-weighted lesions lack specificity (inflammation, edema, axonal loss look alike) (Kumar 2012; Lee 2021)

MWF imaging demonstrates the clinical relevance of multi-exponential T2 mapping, given that myelin quantification could help identify biomarkers for diseases such as MS. MWF technically quantifies the total water trapped between myelin sheaths; it is argued that myelin can be conceptualized as water, its primary composition (Alonso-Ortiz 2015). Conventional T2w images often show hyperintense lesions in MS patients, but these lack specificity and can be indistinguishable from inflammation, edema and axonal loss. MWF holds promise as a biomarker for early microstructural changes in myelin, facilitating early diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression. MWF represents the proportion of the MRI signal attributed to myelin water relative to the total water content, which includes myelin water and intra/extracellular (axonal) water (Lee 2021). Direct imaging of myelin is challenging due to its very short T2. Myelin-associated water has slightly longer T2, typically around 20 ms, which is measurable with standard spin-echo sequences: a more feasible approach to studying myelin. Exercise 3 computes MWF at several TEs with this equation.

Application: quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM)

  • Maps tissue magnetic susceptibility: iron, calcium, oxygen, myelin (Ruetten 2019; Wang 2015)
  • Uses the phase of T2* (gradient echo) data, not only the magnitude (Shmueli 2020)
  • Susceptibility differences cause the field inhomogeneities behind T2′
  • Iron changes in MS and Parkinson’s disease: candidate biomarkers (Stephenson 2014; Chen 2019)
QSM measures variations in tissue magnetic susceptibility (Ruetten 2019; Wang 2015). By assessing differences in susceptibility between tissues, it can quantify paramagnetic substances like iron, as well as diamagnetic substances such as myelin. This has considerable clinical potential: alterations in iron levels have been linked to neurodegenerative diseases like MS (Stephenson 2014) and Parkinson's disease (Chen 2019), making QSM a promising technique for identifying biomarkers. In MRI, the acquired signal is complex, with a magnitude and a phase. Traditional T1, T2 and T2* contrasts only exploit the magnitude; the phase holds valuable information about tissue magnetic susceptibility, which is fundamental for QSM. T2* accounts for both T2 and the relaxation due to field inhomogeneities (T2'), which induce signal dephasing; since susceptibility differences between tissues are a primary cause of these inhomogeneities, phase information is crucial. In QSM, magnitude and phase data from T2* acquisitions together enable the quantification and spatial mapping of susceptibility (Shmueli 2020).

Benefits and pitfalls

Benefits

  • Separates compartments in heterogeneous tissue
  • Gives MWF, for demyelinating diseases such as MS (Alonso-Ortiz 2015)

Pitfalls

  • Longer acquisitions (Kumar 2012)
  • Sensitive to noise: the fit needs high SNR (Dula 2009)
The primary advantage of multi-exponential T2 mapping is improved accuracy in depicting T2 relaxation of complex tissue microstructure, which makes it particularly advantageous for MWF imaging. Challenges: longer acquisition times than mono-exponential models (Kumar 2012), and sensitivity to noise (Dula 2009), which can make accurate fittings challenging.

Summary

Mono-exponential model

Simple and fast, but one T2 per voxel over-simplifies; B1 errors bias it.

Multi-exponential model

Separates compartments, e.g. MWF; needs more time and SNR.

T2*

T2 + field inhomogeneities; gradient echo; its phase gives QSM.

Which model: depends on the application and the tissue.

In conclusion, the choice of mono-exponential versus multi-exponential will depend on the specific clinical or research application as well as the complexity of the tissues being studied. Mono-exponential T2 mapping offers simplicity and efficiency, while multi-exponential T2 mapping provides a comprehensive and accurate characterization of tissue properties, particularly in heterogeneous or pathological tissues.
