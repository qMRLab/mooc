T2 and T2*

T2: spin–spin relaxation

Spins dephase through interactions with their neighbours, so the transverse magnetization decays.

T2*: T2 + field inhomogeneities

Spins also precess at slightly different speeds, fan out, and the signal decays faster.

  • Macroscopic: metallic implants, air–tissue interfaces
  • Microscopic: susceptibility differences between tissues

(Chavhan 2009; Cohen-Adad 2014)

T2 relaxation, also known as the transverse or spin-spin relaxation, is characterized by the dephasing of spins, leading to a reduction of the total magnetization in the x-y plane. The T2 value represents the upper limit of the signal decay time under ideal imaging conditions. In practice, magnetic field inhomogeneities, macroscopic (metallic implants, air-tissue interfaces) or microscopic (susceptibility differences between tissues), cause the transverse magnetization to decay faster. When considering this phenomenon, we refer to the transverse relaxation as T2*. The animation: after a 90° pulse, M (in the rotating frame, so without the precession) shrinks with T2 = 100 ms; the signal |Mxy| decays as e^(−t/T2). Tick “Field inhomogeneities” and play again: M is now a fan of spins at slightly different frequencies (red faster, blue slower; a Lorentzian spread with T2′ = 30 ms), which spread out while each still shrinks with T2, so their sum decays faster, with T2* = 23 ms (1/T2* = 1/T2 + 1/T2′, Eq. 3.3); the T2 curve stays dashed for comparison. Why map T2?

  • A T2 map is an image of T2 relaxation times, with insights into tissue composition
  • Sensitive to microstructural changes associated with disease: iron accumulation, myelination, inflammation (Dortch 2020)
  • A promising modality for clinical and research applications
  • Newer techniques go beyond the conventional ones developed for NMR, e.g. MR fingerprinting: several parameters from a single acquisition (Ma 2013; Hamilton 2017)
T2 mapping offers images of T2 relaxation times (T2 maps) providing valuable insights into tissue composition. Given that T2 relaxation is sensitive to specific microstructural changes associated with diseases, such as iron accumulation, myelination and inflammation (Dortch 2020), it is considered a promising modality for clinical and research applications. Novel techniques have emerged beyond the conventional ones developed for NMR, such as MR fingerprinting (Ma 2013), a fast relaxation mapping technique that uses a single image acquisition to quantify multiple parameters (Hamilton 2017).

What this chapter covers

Mono-exponential

The voxel's signal arises from a single tissue compartment.

One T2 per voxel, fitted from a multi-echo spin echo acquisition.

Multi-exponential

Several tissues contribute to one voxel's signal.

Applications such as myelin water fraction (MWF) imaging.

T2*

Fundamentals of T2* mapping, and how its decay curve differs from T2.

Related application: quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM).

Commonly used T2-mapping techniques are split into two categories: mono-exponential methods, which consider that the voxel's signal arises from a single tissue compartment, and multi-exponential methods, where various tissues that contribute to a single voxel's signal are considered. The chapter covers signal modeling and data fitting for both, their benefits and pitfalls, an overview of applications (e.g. MWF imaging), and the fundamentals of T2* mapping and how its signal decay curve can differ from that of T2 relaxation.

T2 mapping vs T2-weighted imaging

T2-weighted (T2w)

  • The standard practice in clinical MRI
  • Tissues give signal according to their T2: differences in T2 provide contrast
  • Qualitative: interpretation depends on the observer

T2 mapping

  • Quantitative: fits multiple data points to a decay curve to calculate T2
  • Objective: disease progression monitoring, novel biomarkers
  • Consistent comparisons across imaging protocols: suited to longitudinal and multi-institutional studies (Cheng 2012)
T2w images are considered qualitative, as they rely on differences in T2 relaxation of various tissues to provide contrast rather than actual quantitative tissue measurements, making their interpretation dependent on the observer. T2 mapping directly measures the T2 relaxation time of tissues by fitting multiple data points to a decay curve. Its objective nature makes it promising for disease progression monitoring and the identification of novel biomarkers, and it allows consistent comparisons across imaging protocols, enhancing reproducibility for longitudinal and multi-institutional studies (Cheng 2012).

Method 1 · Mono-exponential

Mono-exponential T2 Mapping

A single fitted T2 value for each voxel

Mono-exponential T2 mapping fits one decay curve, and one T2 value, per voxel.

Acquiring a decay curve

  • The simplest technique: several images with a single-echo sequence at different echo times (TE), then fit T2 to the decaying signal (Milford 2015)
    • Not typically used in vivo: prohibitively long acquisition times
  • Multi-spin echo (MSE) sequences, based on the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse train (Carr 1954; Meiboom 1958)
    • Multiple 180° refocusing pulses generate multiple echoes in a single acquisition (Fatemi 2020; Milford 2015)
  • Substantially shorter scan time than separate single-echo acquisitions: MSE is the sequence of choice in clinical settings
The simplest technique for T2 mapping is the acquisition of multiple images using a single-echo sequence with different TEs, then fitting T2 from the decaying signal (Milford 2015). It is typically not employed in vivo due to prohibitively long acquisition times. Multi-spin echo sequences, based on the CPMG pulse train, employ multiple 180° refocusing pulses to generate multiple echoes within a single acquisition, substantially reducing scan time and making MSE the sequence of choice in clinical settings.

Carr–Purcell, and the Meiboom–Gill improvement

Carr–Purcell

  • A 90° pulse, then a series of 180° pulses along the same axis (e.g. x)
  • Mitigates diffusion effects by preventing phase accumulation
  • But imperfections in the 180° pulses accumulate: Mxy is lost faster than expected, and T2 is inaccurate (Brown 2014)

Meiboom–Gill

  • The 90° pulse along one axis (e.g. x), the 180° pulses along a perpendicular axis (e.g. y)
  • This phase cycling distributes the errors so they average out over time
  • A more stable and accurate echo train (Brown 2014)
The original Carr-Purcell method applies a 90-degree pulse followed by a series of 180-degree pulses along the same axis, designed to mitigate diffusion effects by preventing phase accumulation. However, imperfections in the 180-degree pulses accumulate, resulting in a faster than expected loss of transverse magnetization and inaccurate T2 measurements (Brown 2014). The Meiboom-Gill improvement applies the initial 90-degree pulse along one axis and the subsequent 180-degree pulses along a perpendicular axis; this phase cycling distributes errors such that they average out over time.

The mono-exponential assumption

  • Homogeneity within each voxel is assumed implicitly: one T2 decay time across the tissue
  • So a single fitted T2 value for each voxel in the image
  • Widely used, but shown to be insufficient for proper estimation of tissue T2: a voxel's signal often arises from multiple components with different T2 values (Graham 1996)

Multi-exponential methods, and their use for myelin water fraction imaging, come later in the chapter.

To fit the data using the mono-exponential model, homogeneity within each voxel is assumed implicitly, implying a consistent T2 decay time across the tissue. This results in a single fitted T2 value for each voxel. Although widely used, the mono-exponential model has been shown to be insufficient for proper estimation of tissue T2, given that the signal in a voxel often arises from multiple tissue components with different T2 values (Graham 1996).

Signal modelling

The decay of the transverse magnetization is exponential, from the transverse component of the Bloch equations:

$$M_{xy}(TE) = M_z(0^{-})\,e^{-TE/T_{2}}\qquad\text{(3.1)}$$
  • Mz(0−) is the longitudinal magnetization immediately before the 90° excitation pulse
  • Assumes the measured signal is proportional to Mxy, and that Mz(0−) is constant regardless of TE (Dortch 2020)
The decay of the transverse magnetization is exponential and can be derived from the transverse component of the Bloch equations. Mz(0-) is the longitudinal magnetization immediately preceding the 90 degree excitation pulse. Using this equation assumes that the measured signal is proportional to Mxy, and that Mz(0-) remains constant regardless of echo time (Dortch 2020). T2 and T1

  • In NMR physics, T2 must be equal to or shorter than 2 T1 (Levitt 2008)
  • T2 can exceed T1 in very rare cases (Traficante 1991)
  • In living organisms, T2 is always shorter than T1
In NMR physics, it has been shown that T2 relaxation times must be equal to or shorter than 2 T1 (Levitt 2008); however, T2 can exceed T1 in very rare cases (Traficante 1991). In living organisms, T2 is always shorter than T1.

Data fitting

The mono-exponential T2 signal decay:

$$S(TE) = S_{0}\,e^{-TE/T_{2}}\qquad\text{(3.2)}$$

S0 is the signal intensity immediately following the excitation pulse (Dortch 2020; Milford 2015).

The T2 signal decay for the mono-exponential model is S(TE) = S0 exp(−TE/T2), where S0 is the signal intensity immediately following the excitation pulse (Dortch 2020; Milford 2015).

Stimulated echoes

  • B1 inhomogeneities and RF pulse imperfections make refocusing pulses deviate from the ideal 180°
  • This generates additional echoes, known as stimulated or spurious echoes
  • They contaminate the signal decay: erroneous T2 estimates (McPhee 2018)

Fitting with improved accuracy

  • Use only the even-numbered echoes (Focke 2011; Kim 2009)
  • Or discard the first echo (Biasiolli 2013; Milford 2015)
In practice, B1 inhomogeneities and RF pulse imperfections can influence the T2 decay curve. Refocusing pulses deviating from 180 degrees generate stimulated or spurious echoes that contaminate the signal decay and result in erroneous T2 estimations (McPhee 2018). Some studies have shown that fitting accuracy can be improved either by using only even-numbered echoes (Focke 2011; Kim 2009), or by discarding the first echo (Biasiolli 2013; Milford 2015).

T2*: faster than T2

  • So far, Mxy decays exponentially with TE / T2 (Eq. 3.1)
  • In practice, B0 inhomogeneities cause a faster loss of the transverse signal: T2* relaxation (Figure 3.3)
$$\frac{1}{T_{2}^{*}} = \frac{1}{T_{2}} + \frac{1}{T_{2}'}\qquad\text{(3.3)}$$
  • T2′ quantifies the part of the relaxation that is due to magnetic field inhomogeneities (Brown 2014)
Until now, we have assumed that Mxy decays exponentially with TE divided by T2. In practice, other factors such as B0 inhomogeneities cause a more rapid loss of the transverse signal, a faster decay referred to as T2* relaxation. T2' quantifies the portion of relaxation which is due to magnetic field inhomogeneities. (The chapter's text here points to Eq. 3.3 and Figure 3.1; the T2 model is Eq. 3.1 and the T2* curves are Figure 3.3.)

T2′ and measuring T2*

  • T2′ mapping, by removing the T2 effect from T2*, could help brain disease diagnosis:
    • quantifying blood oxygenation (Lee 2014)
    • predicting infarct growth in acute stroke (Siemonsen 2008)
  • T2* is fitted with the same methods as T2:
$$S(TE) = S_{0}\,e^{-TE/T_{2}^{*}}\qquad\text{(3.4)}$$
  • Acquired with gradient echo (GRE) sequences, which are sensitive to field inhomogeneities, unlike the spin echo sequences used for T2 (Cohen-Adad 2014; Markl 2012)
  • SAGE (spin and gradient echo) acquires T2 and T2* simultaneously (Stokes 2014)
Some studies suggest that T2' mapping could offer valuable insights for brain disease diagnosis, notably by quantifying blood oxygenation levels (Lee 2014) and predicting infarct growth in acute stroke patients (Siemonsen 2008). The T2* decay can be fitted using the same methods as T2. Unlike T2 mapping, which uses spin echo type sequences, T2* mapping is performed with gradient echo sequences, as they are sensitive to magnetic field inhomogeneities. Specialized sequences such as SAGE enable simultaneous acquisition of both T2 and T2*. Note: the book's Eq. 3.4 shows T2 in the exponent; the slide uses T2*, as the text describes.

Noise

  • Noise makes the T2 decay harder to fit accurately, a problem when clinical contexts need highly precise T2 values
  • Particularly for pixel-wise T2 mapping: SNR is much lower than with region-of-interest (ROI) approaches (Sandino 2015)

Next: a single-voxel fit, true T2 = 109 ms, at four SNR levels.

In MRI, noise in the data can make it harder to accurately fit the T2 decay curve, which is problematic given the necessity for highly precise T2 values in clinical contexts. This is particularly pronounced with pixel-wise T2 mapping, as the SNR is much lower than in ROI T2 mapping approaches (Sandino 2015).

How many echoes, and when?

  • The number of echoes depends on echo spacing, the risk of heating the sample, and processing low-SNR data (Shrager 1998)
  • The Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB): the smallest possible variance of an unbiased estimator, given the noise in the data (Cavassila 2001)
    • Gives the optimal number of echoes to fit the decay accurately (Jones 1996)
  • Optimized echo times for a predetermined range of expected T2 values beat uniformly spaced echo times in accuracy (Shrager 1998)
The number of echoes is influenced by the need for adequate spacing between echoes, the potential risk of heating the sample, and the challenges of processing low-SNR data (Shrager 1998). The CRLB estimates the smallest possible variance of an unbiased estimator given the noise in the data (Cavassila 2001); from it, the optimal number of echoes can be determined (Jones 1996). Shrager 1998 introduced a method that optimizes the selection of echo times based on a predetermined range of expected T2 values, with superior accuracy to uniformly spaced echo times.

Benefits and pitfalls

Benefits

  • Simple, straightforward implementation: a convenient and efficient T2 fit
  • Multi-echo spin echo (MESE) sequences significantly reduce acquisition time (Fatemi 2020; Milford 2015)

Pitfalls

  • One T2 per voxel over-simplifies the microstructure: a problem for complex tissues with several T2 components in one voxel
  • MESE is sensitive to RF pulse imperfections: B1 inhomogeneities and reduced flip angles overestimate T2 (Fatemi 2020)
The main benefit of mono-exponential T2 mapping is its simplicity and straightforward implementation; MESE sequences significantly reduce acquisition time. Drawbacks: assuming a single T2 per voxel tends to over-simplify the tissue microstructure, which is particularly problematic for tissues with complex microstructure. MESE sequences are also sensitive to RF pulse imperfections: B1 inhomogeneities and reduced flip angles have been shown to overestimate T2 with mono-exponential methods (Fatemi 2020).

Method 2 · Multi-exponential

Multi-exponential T2 Mapping

The T2 contribution of each tissue compartment within a voxel

Multi-exponential T2 mapping considers the T2 relaxation contribution of each tissue compartment within a voxel.

One voxel, several compartments

  • The mono-exponential curve is a single-compartment tissue model: every component in a voxel has the same T2
  • But a voxel may contain different tissue compartments with different T2 times, so this assumption can give inaccurate fits
  • Example: a voxel at a tissue boundary in the brain holding both CSF and gray matter, the partial volume effect
  • Multi-exponential T2 mapping compartmentalizes the tissues inside a voxel, with applications such as myelin water fraction imaging
The mono-exponential curve uses a single compartment tissue model: all tissue components in a voxel are assumed to have the same T2. In practice this can result in inaccurate fittings, as a voxel may contain different tissue compartments with different T2 times, e.g. a voxel at a tissue boundary containing both CSF and gray matter (partial volume effect). Multi-exponential T2 mapping considers the T2 contribution of each compartment within a voxel, which is useful in applications such as myelin water fraction imaging.

Signal modelling: a sum of exponentials

The transverse magnetization acquired at different TEs, as a sum of exponential decays:

$$M_{xy}(TE) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} M_{z,i}(0^{-})\,e^{-TE/T_{2,i}}\qquad\text{(3.5)}$$
  • Each term is the contribution of the ith tissue component to the overall decay (Collewet 2022; Dortch 2020)

Next: a single voxel of myelin water (MW) and intra/extracellular water (IEW), with mono-exponential curves for each and the multi-exponential curve for both.

For multi-exponential T2 mapping, the transverse magnetization acquired at different TEs is modelled as a sum of exponential decays, where each term represents the contribution of the ith tissue component (Collewet 2022; Dortch 2020). Using a multi-exponential model rather than mono-exponential for complex tissues like myelin enables more precise quantification of T2 within each voxel.

Data fitting

Eq. 3.5 in terms of the initial amplitude Si of each tissue component:

$$S(TE) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} S_{i}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,i}}\qquad\text{(3.6)}$$

Si: initial signal amplitude of the ith component; T2,i: its T2.

For myelin water (MW) and intra/extracellular water (IEW), as in Figure 3.5:

$$S(TE)_{MWI} = S_{MW}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,MW}} + S_{IEW}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,IEW}}\qquad\text{(3.7)}$$
To fit the data, Eq. 3.5 is rewritten in terms of the initial amplitude Si of each tissue component, with T2,i the T2 of that component. The multi-exponential signal of Figure 3.5 is the combination of the myelin water and intra/extracellular water signals (Eq. 3.7).

Application: myelin water fraction (MWF) imaging

  • Demonstrates the clinical relevance of multi-exponential T2 mapping (Kumar 2012; MacKay 1994)
  • Myelin quantification could help identify biomarkers for diseases such as multiple sclerosis (MS)
  • MWF does not directly measure myelin: it quantifies the water trapped between the myelin sheaths, the primary component of myelin (Alonso-Ortiz 2015)

Why not T2-weighted images?

  • Hyperintense lesions in MS lack specificity: indistinguishable from inflammation, edema, axonal loss (Alonso-Ortiz 2015; Lee 2021)
  • MWF could show early microstructural changes in myelin, for early diagnosis and monitoring
MWF imaging demonstrates the clinical relevance of multi-exponential T2 mapping, given that myelin quantification could help identify biomarkers for diseases such as MS. MWF technically quantifies the total water trapped between myelin sheaths; it is argued that myelin can be conceptualized as water, its primary composition (Alonso-Ortiz 2015). Conventional T2w images often show hyperintense lesions in MS patients, but these lack specificity and can be indistinguishable from inflammation, edema and axonal loss. MWF holds promise as a biomarker for early microstructural changes in myelin, facilitating early diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression.

The myelin water fraction

The proportion of the MRI signal from myelin water, relative to the total water content:

$$MWF = \frac{S_{MW}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,MW}}}{S_{MW}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,MW}} + S_{IEW}\,e^{-TE/T_{2,IEW}}}\qquad\text{(3.8)}$$
  • Total water: myelin water (MW) + intra/extracellular water (IEW), also called axonal water (Lee 2021)
  • Myelin itself is hard to image directly: its T2 is very short
  • Myelin-associated water has a slightly longer T2, around 20 ms (Lee 2021): still short, but measurable with standard spin echo sequences
MWF represents the proportion of the MRI signal attributed to myelin water relative to the total water content, which includes myelin water and intra/extracellular (axonal) water (Lee 2021). Direct imaging of myelin is challenging due to its very short T2. Myelin-associated water has slightly longer T2, typically around 20 ms, which is measurable with standard spin-echo sequences: a more feasible approach to studying myelin. Application: quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM)

  • QSM measures variations in tissue magnetic susceptibility (Ruetten 2019; Wang 2015)
  • Quantifies paramagnetic and diamagnetic substances such as iron, calcium, oxygen and myelin (Ruetten 2019): insights into tissue physiology and integrity
  • Altered iron levels are linked to multiple sclerosis (Stephenson 2014) and Parkinson’s disease (Chen 2019): candidate biomarkers for neurodegenerative disorders
QSM measures variations in tissue magnetic susceptibility (Ruetten 2019; Wang 2015). By assessing differences in susceptibility between tissues, it can quantify paramagnetic substances like iron, as well as diamagnetic substances such as myelin. This has considerable clinical potential: alterations in iron levels have been linked to neurodegenerative diseases like MS (Stephenson 2014) and Parkinson's disease (Chen 2019), making QSM a promising technique for identifying biomarkers.

QSM: the phase holds the susceptibility

  • The MRI signal is complex: magnitude and phase
  • T1, T2 and T2* contrasts use only the magnitude; the phase carries information about tissue magnetic susceptibility
  • T2* includes T2′, the dephasing from field inhomogeneities, caused mainly by susceptibility differences between tissues (Ruetten 2019)
  • QSM combines the magnitude and phase of T2* acquisitions to map susceptibility within tissues (Shmueli 2020)

Next: susceptibility and B0 in a brain, simulated at 7 T.

In MRI, the acquired signal is complex, with a magnitude and a phase. Traditional T1, T2 and T2* contrasts only exploit the magnitude; the phase holds valuable information about tissue magnetic susceptibility, which is fundamental for QSM. T2* accounts for both T2 and the relaxation due to field inhomogeneities (T2'), which induce signal dephasing; since susceptibility differences between tissues are a primary cause of these inhomogeneities, phase information is crucial. In QSM, magnitude and phase data from T2* acquisitions together enable the quantification and spatial mapping of susceptibility (Shmueli 2020).

Benefits and pitfalls

Benefits

  • More accurate T2 for complex, heterogeneous tissue: each voxel is multi-compartmental, each tissue with its own T2
  • Particularly suited to MWF imaging, to understand demyelinating diseases such as MS (Alonso-Ortiz 2015)

Pitfalls

  • More complex than mono-exponential: longer acquisition times (Kumar 2012)
  • Sensitive to noise (Dula 2009), which can make accurate fits challenging
The primary advantage of multi-exponential T2 mapping is improved accuracy in depicting T2 relaxation of complex tissue microstructure, which makes it particularly advantageous for MWF imaging. Challenges: longer acquisition times than mono-exponential models (Kumar 2012), and sensitivity to noise (Dula 2009), which can make accurate fittings challenging.

Summary

Mono-exponential

Simple and efficient; fast with multi-echo spin echo.

One T2 per voxel over-simplifies tissue; B1 errors overestimate T2.

Multi-exponential

Comprehensive and accurate for heterogeneous or pathological tissue, e.g. MWF.

Longer acquisitions; sensitive to noise.

T2*

T2 plus field inhomogeneities (T2′); measured with gradient echo.

With the phase, the basis of QSM.

The choice of mono- or multi-exponential depends on the clinical or research application and the complexity of the tissues studied.

In conclusion, the choice of mono-exponential versus multi-exponential will depend on the specific clinical or research application as well as the complexity of the tissues being studied. Mono-exponential T2 mapping offers simplicity and efficiency, while multi-exponential T2 mapping provides a comprehensive and accurate characterization of tissue properties, particularly in heterogeneous or pathological tissues.
