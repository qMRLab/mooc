Tutorial for the game's keys. A single on-resonance spin with T1 and T2 infinite, starting along +z. Holding an arrow key turns on B1 along that axis (shown in orange, labelled B1) and the magnetization rotates around it by the right-hand rule, at 90° per second; releasing stops it. Try: hold → for one second to tip M from +z onto −y (a 90° about +x). Then hold ↑ or ↓: M lies on the B1 axis (−y), so it does not move. Hold → again and M carries on around the yz plane to −z. What we saw · 1

A pulse’s phase is the axis M turns around

  • Perpendicular to the axis, M sweeps a circle; otherwise, a cone.
  • Along the axis, M doesn’t move.
Lesson of the controls slide: the pulse phase (+x, −x, +y, −y) is the rotation axis. The cone is the key picture for what follows: a pulse acts differently on magnetization depending on where M sits relative to the pulse axis, which is why the axis of the refocusing pulses matters once they are not exactly 180° (CP versus CPMG in Exercise 2-2). In full: The phase sets where B1 points in the transverse plane: +x, −x, +y or −y. M turns around that axis (right-hand rule); −x turns it the opposite way to +x. Same angle, different axis, different result: from +z, 90° about +x puts M on −y; 90° about +y puts it on +x. M sweeps a great circle only when it is perpendicular to the axis. Otherwise it sweeps a cone around the axis: after 45° about +x, M is halfway between +z and −y, and a pulse about +y swings its tip around a cone. M along the axis doesn’t move at all: after 90° about +x, M is on −y, and a pulse about ±y leaves it there. (Exercise 2-2 comes back to this.)

One 180° with free timing. The 90° fires at t = 0; the 180° fires the moment an arrow is pressed, at t_180. The echo forms at 2·t_180: the spins have fanned out for t_180, the 180° flips their phases, and they take as long again to come back together. What we saw · 2

A 180° refocuses the fan

  • The echo forms at TE = 2 × t180.
  • Only T2 decay is lost: e−TE/T2.
Lesson of the spin echo slide. The spread of frequencies (the fan) is reversible and is undone by the 180°; T2 decay is not. In full: After the 90°, spins at slightly different frequencies fan out and the signal fades. The 180° flips the fan: spins that were ahead are now behind, so they come back together. The echo forms as long after the 180° as the 180° came after the 90°: TE = 2 × t180. The echo is smaller only because of T2, e−TE/T2: the 180° undoes the fanning out, not the T2 decay.

A stimulated echo

Question: delay the third pulse. Which marker moves with it, and by how much?

Three 90° pulses: the first on its own at t = 0, then one per arrow press, whenever you like (spaced out, say near 20 and 50 ms, the echoes stand apart). Markers show every echo they will make: spin echoes SE, and the stimulated echo STE.

Three 90° pulses, the last two fired by the arrow keys whenever the class likes; pulses near 20 and 50 ms keep the echoes apart (the fan is a bit slower on this slide, ±200° by 10 ms, and the clock runs at 4 ms per second). After the second 90°, part of the fanned-out signal is tipped onto z, where it keeps a record of each spin’s phase (as a pattern of Mz) and only relaxes with T1. The third 90° brings it back, mirrored, and it refocuses τ after that pulse, where τ is the gap between the first two: the stimulated echo (STE). It is at most half of the signal. The spin echoes (SE) come at twice the spacings: 2·t2 (pulses 1 and 2), 2·t3 − 2·t2 (refocused again by the third pulse), 2·t3 (pulses 1 and 3). Try waiting longer before the third pulse: the STE moves with it, always τ later, and barely shrinks (T1 = 1000 ms), while the spin echoes fade with T2 = 80 ms.

What we saw · 3

Three pulses also make a stimulated echo

  • It comes τ after the third pulse: delay that pulse and the STE follows it, while the spin echoes move twice as far.
  • It waits along z, relaxing with T1; with 90° pulses it carries up to half the signal.
Lesson of the stimulated echo slide. The next slide compares the two echoes side by side. In full: The second 90° parks part of the fanned-out signal along z, keeping each spin’s phase as a pattern of Mz. The third 90° brings it back, and it refocuses τ after that pulse (τ = the gap between the first two): the stimulated echo. While parked along z it relaxes with T1, not T2, so it can outlast the spin echoes. With 90° pulses it carries up to half of the signal; the spin echoes come at the mirror times as before.

An imperfect 180° is part 180°, part 90°

A refocusing pulse of angle α acts on the fanned-out transverse signal in three ways at once: it refocuses a share sin²(α/2) (like a 180°: spin echo), leaves cos²(α/2) alone (like a 0°), and tips part onto z (like a 90°: ½ sin α of it). What it tips onto z, the next pulse’s 90° part brings back as a stimulated echo. At 180° the bars read 100%, 0%, 0%: only spin echoes. At 160°: 97%, 3%, 17%. At 120°: 75%, 25%, 43%. At 90°: 50%, 50%, 50%, the stimulated-echo slide. The marker sizes follow the same rules, with T2 = 80 ms while transverse and T1 = 1000 ms along z. This is why a train of imperfect 180°s (Exercise 2-2) has stimulated echoes in every echo after the first.

What we saw · 4

An imperfect 180° is part 180°, part 90°

  • Its 90° part parks signal along z; the next pulse brings it back as a stimulated echo.
  • Only a perfect 180° gives pure spin echoes.
Lesson of the imperfect-180° slide. B1 inhomogeneity and slice profiles make refocusing angles below 180° the norm in practice. In full: A refocusing pulse of angle α acts like a 180° on sin²(α/2) of the signal, like a 0° on cos²(α/2), and like a 90° on ½ sin α. Its 90° part parks signal along z, and the next pulse brings it back: from the second refocusing pulse on, stimulated echoes appear. At 160°: 97% refocused, 3% left alone, 17% to z. At 120°: 75%, 25%, 43%. Only a perfect 180° gives pure spin echoes.

The bridge to Exercise 2-2. With the 90° at 0 and refocusing pulses at τ, 3τ, 5τ (τ = 10 ms), every spin echo and every stimulated echo forms at 2τ, 4τ, 6τ: they add up in the same echo, and the signal measured at each echo time is a mix of both (amber markers, SE+STE). That is a multi-echo spin echo (CPMG) train with imperfect refocusing pulses, the setting of Exercise 2-2. Timed by hand, the pulses are never quite even and the echoes split apart again.

What we saw · 5

Evenly spaced, the echoes coincide

  • In an echo train, each echo mixes spin and stimulated echoes.
  • Their parts can add or partly cancel.
Lesson of the evenly spaced slide, and the bridge to Exercise 2-2. In full: With refocusing pulses at τ, 3τ, 5τ, …, every spin echo and every stimulated echo forms at 2τ, 4τ, 6τ, … So each echo of a multi-echo spin echo train is a mix of spin and stimulated echoes, unless the refocusing angle is exactly 180°. The parts meeting in one echo can add or partly cancel, depending on their phases: with 120° pulses at 10, 30 and 50 ms, the echo at 60 ms is 42%, not the 70% their sizes add up to. This mix is what Exercise 2-2 measures, and why it biases T2.

Lessons

On echoes

  1. Phase = rotation axis: a circle if M is perpendicular to it, a cone otherwise.
  2. Spin echo at 2 × t180: only T2 decay is lost.
  3. Stimulated echo: waits along z (T1), comes τ after the third pulse.
  4. Imperfect 180° = part 180°, part 90°: stimulated echoes.
  5. Evenly spaced pulses: every echo mixes spin and stimulated echoes.
