📖 B0 Mapping

Why map B0?

Image reconstruction assumes a perfectly uniform B0. It never is.

A non-uniform B0 causes

  • Signal loss (T2*)
  • Geometric distortion (EPI)
  • Failed fat saturation
  • Broad MRS lines

A B0 map lets you

  • Shim the field
  • Unwarp EPI (FUGUE)
  • Correct T2* decay
  • Compute QSM

Measured at 3 T, after shimming: one axial slice, dual-echo GRE.

B0 sets the precession frequency and the bulk magnetization; the RF coils are tuned to it. Reconstruction assumes it is perfectly homogeneous. Inhomogeneity leads to signal loss, distortions (Jezzard 1995), poor fat saturation (Anzai 1992) and broader MRS linewidths; in extreme cases no image at all. B0 maps are used for active shimming, retrospective distortion correction (FSL FUGUE, Jenkinson 2012), recovery of T2* signal decay (An 2002) and QSM. The map: the 2013 Stikov 3 T session (the B1 deck's dataset), the qMT protocol's B0 map (mtspgr_b0, Boudreau, Stikov & Pike, MRM 2018): a two-point phase-difference GRE, TR 25 ms, 7°, one 5 mm slice, 2 mm pixels. Complex phase difference divided by 2π·ΔTE with ΔTE = 2.24 ms (the data and the original pipeline agree on 2.24 ms; the paper reports 4/8.48 ms). Even after shimming the field varies across the slice by roughly −30 to +10 Hz, lowest frontally toward the sinuses. The scalp is masked: fat's chemical shift is not field.

A B0 map is the difference between the current field and the expected field, in T, Hz or ppm (Eq. 5.1). Frequency and field are linked by the Larmor equation f = γB/2π (γ/2π = 42.58 MHz/T), so at 3 T: f0 ≈ 127.7 MHz and 1 ppm ≈ 128 Hz ≈ 3 µT. Figure 5.1: magnitude, phase and computed field map of a brain at 3 T.

Where ΔB0 comes from

The scanner

  • Magnet imperfections: < 1 ppm, fixed by passive shimming
  • Slow drift from heating

The subject

  • Tissue magnetic susceptibility χ
$$\Delta B_z = B_0\,(\Delta\chi \otimes d)$$

The dipole kernel d (image space) and D (k-space, as used in QSM).

Hardware: kilometres of superconducting wire wound within tolerances, plus nearby metal; homogenized at installation by passive shimming (ferromagnetic pieces). Under 1 ppm (Webb 2016). Field probes (e.g. Skope) can measure the field more precisely and quickly than field maps, including eddy currents. Heating during a session causes drift, about 0.4 Hz/min in MRS at 3 T; scanners correct for it. Subject: susceptibility χ, the ability to become magnetized. The field change is proportional to Δχ and to B0, and depends on geometry: a convolution of Δχ with the unit dipole field d = (3cos²θ − 1)/(4π|r|³), θ the angle with B0 (Eq. 5.2, de Rochefort 2008).

Air–tissue interfaces dominate

  • Air (+χ) next to tissue (−χ): largest offsets
  • ΔB0 scales with B0 (here 7 T)
  • Every subject needs its own shim

Menu: cylinders or brain. χ (left), field (middle), field with the slow background removed (right).

A homogeneous empty bore becomes inhomogeneous once a subject is inside, hence active shimming for each subject. Strongest variations at boundaries between air (slightly paramagnetic, +χ) and water/tissue (diamagnetic, −χ). Figure 5.3: susceptibility distributions for nested cylinders and a brain surrounded by air, the simulated B0 at 7 T, and the field with the slowly varying background removed. In the brain the field is dominated by air-tissue boundaries. The book's version shows the brain at higher resolution; this is the notebook's compact version with a menu.

Effect 1: dephasing

  • Different frequencies in a voxel → phases spread → faster decay
  • Demodulating at the wrong frequency → oscillations
$$\frac{1}{T_2^{*}} = \frac{1}{T_2} + \frac{1}{T_2'}$$

Menu: one, two or many frequencies (isochromats).

Excitation off resonance gives a suboptimal flip angle, varying across the image (Wang 2006). On readout the signal is demodulated at the Larmor frequency. One isochromat at exactly that frequency: the T2 decay is recovered. Off resonance: low-frequency oscillations. Many isochromats (or a homogeneous sample in a non-uniform B0): intravoxel phase dispersion and a faster decay, T2*, with 1/T2* = 1/T2 + 1/T2'. Figure 5.4.

Effect 2: wrong frequency, wrong place

  • Frequency encodes position: off-resonance shifts voxels
  • Errors build up along the readout: EPI is worst
  • Also: slice shifts, failed fat sat

Red: a 0.1 mT/m parasitic gradient (0.25 % of the 40 mT/m encoding gradients).

Off-resonance breaks the one-to-one link between frequency and position, so images are distorted; errors compound the further k-space is traversed. Figure 5.5: EPI with bipolar readouts (blue) and the same with a constant gradient added in the phase-encoding direction (red), one in five phase-encoding lines shown. RF pulses are affected too: during slice selection the field is Gz·z + B0 + ΔB0(x,y,z), so the slice is distorted or shifted; frequency-selective pulses such as fat saturation can fail. Also ringing, blurring and ghosting. All are reduced by shimming, which needs a B0 map.

Phase grows linearly with time

$$\phi(\mathbf r,t) = \phi_0(\mathbf r) + \gamma\,\Delta B_0(\mathbf r)\,t$$
  • Slope = γΔB0: the field map
  • φ0 (coil, RF) is unknown → at least two echoes

Play; the menu switches between laboratory and rotating frame. In the rotating frame the on-resonance spin (blue) stands still.

Figure 5.6: a spin at the Larmor frequency (blue) and one at a lower frequency (red), in the transverse plane and as phase versus time. Eq. 5.3 (rotating frame, ignoring transients such as eddy currents): φ = φ0 + γΔB0·t. φ0 is an initial phase offset (coil, local conductivity/permittivity), generally unknown and non-zero, so two phase images are needed.

Dual echo: subtract two phases

$$\Delta B_0 = \frac{\phi(TE_2) - \phi(TE_1)}{\gamma\,\Delta TE}$$
  • φ0 cancels
  • Complex difference keeps Δφ in (−π, π]: \(\angle\big(e^{i\phi_2}\,e^{-i\phi_1}\big)\)

Phase at longer echo times

  • Measured phase lives in (−π, π]
  • Sharp jumps are phase wraps
  • Longer TE → more wraps

Phase images come from real and imaginary data: ∠ = atan2(imag, real)

Slider: echo time.

Figure 5.7: brain phase at progressively longer echo times. At one voxel the phase changes linearly; sharp lines are phase wraps, because phase is only defined over −π to π. Wraps also appear spatially, and more of them at longer TE. Not every scanner exports phase by default; it can be turned on, or computed from real/imaginary data with arctan2 (Eq. 5.4).

Choosing the echo times

  • Phase noise: \(\sigma_{\phi} = 1/\mathrm{SNR}_{mag}\) → short TE1
  • Longer ΔTE: more precise field
  • Shorter ΔTE: fewer wraps
  • Fat and water in phase at 3 T: ΔTE ≈ 2.34 ms
  • GRE, monopolar readouts, high bandwidth (not EPI)

No wraps in Δφ if

$$\Delta TE < \frac{1}{2\,\Delta f_{max}}$$

Benefits and pitfalls. At least two phase images, since φ0 is unknown. GRE, or spin echo with an asymmetric second echo shifted 1-2 ms. High bandwidth, thin slices and multi-shot reduce B0 artifacts in the field map itself, so EPI is generally avoided (Akcakaya 2022). Monopolar readouts avoid misregistration between odd and even echoes. σphase = 1/SNRmag (Eq. 5.7, Brown 2014), so TE1 is short. ΔTE: fat has a ~3.35 ppm shift, which is mistaken for field; choose ΔTE so fat and water are in phase (~2.34 ms at 3 T). Longer ΔTE: better estimate if SNR allows; shorter: fewer wraps. Table 5.1 (calculator): with offsets up to ±Δf_max, the phase difference 2πΔf·ΔTE stays within ±π if ΔTE < 1/(2Δf_max). At 1 ppm this reproduces the book's table (3.91 ms at 3 T, 7.82 ms at 1.5 T). Eddy currents from fast switching between echoes: one echo per RF pulse avoids them, at twice the scan time. Note: this calculator also answers the chapter's first exercise.

Phase wrapping

  • We measure phase modulo 2π
  • True phase = measured + 2πn: find n

Two families

  • Temporal: across echoes
  • Spatial: across neighbouring voxels

Wrapped phase (blue) and true phase (red) along one line.

Phase can only be measured from −π to +π. When it crosses ±π a 2π jump appears although the true phase varies smoothly. Spins can turn more than one revolution, so unwrapping is needed. Temporal unwrapping uses several time points, assuming the change between them is below π. Spatial unwrapping assumes neighbouring voxels vary smoothly. Figure 5.11: a linearly varying phase, wrapped.

Temporal unwrapping

  • Assume the phase changes by < π between echoes
  • Add or remove 2π where it jumps
  • Fit a line: slope = γΔB0

One voxel at four echo times: measured (blue), unwrapped (red), linear fit (green).

With two or more time points whose phase differs by at most π, the complex difference is free of wraps (Eq. 5.8: Δf0 = Δφ/(2πΔTE)). Table 5.1 gives the largest ΔTE for 1 ppm at each field strength; it gets shorter at higher field. If ΔTE is longer, or the field worse than expected, the difference image wraps too and spatial unwrapping is needed. Figure 5.10: the 4th echo has wrapped; subtracting 2π recovers the true phase; the slope of the fit is the field map value.

Spatial unwrapping

  • Neighbours vary smoothly: region growing from a seed
  • The seed sets the answer: solutions differ by 2nπ

Drag the divider: wrapped phase (left), the three possible unwrapped solutions (right).

Spatial unwrapping looks for offsets larger than 2π between neighbours, typically with region growing. Figure 5.12: a phase that varies non-linearly in space. Unwrapping gives several solutions, 2nπ apart, depending on which part of the signal is taken as the true phase. The book's Dash app is redrawn here.

Unwrapping in 2D

  • Needs one connected region, and noise masked out
  • Pick n so the mean phase in the ROI is ≈ 0 (after shimming)
  • Δφ maps unwrap more reliably than single echoes

Simulated wrapped (left) and unwrapped (right) phase. More wraps = more inhomogeneity.

Region growing needs a single region, or there can be 2nπ offsets between regions, and usually a threshold so noise is not unwrapped. Figure 5.13. Ambiguity: when unwrapping one phase volume, choose n so the mean phase in the ROI is close to 0, assuming a good frequency shim. Not fully robust (receive coil, RF pulse and eddy currents add phase), but phase differences are better behaved because constant offsets cancel. With multiple echoes, spatial and temporal unwrapping can be combined, and well-chosen echo times remove the 2nπ ambiguity.

Phase singularities

  • Two paths from A to B cross a different number of wraps
  • No unwrapper can give one right answer
  • Cause: poor coil combination

Fix the coil combination, not the unwrapper

Phase singularities (poles, open-ended fringe lines). Counting wraps: −π to +π (black to white) is +1, the reverse −1. All paths between two points should cross the same net number of wraps; around a singularity they don't. Figure 5.14: from A to B the left path crosses no wrap (3π/4 to −3π/4), the right one crosses one (3π/4 to −3π/4 + 2π). Some techniques mitigate this, but the main solution is to combine the coil channels correctly.

PRELUDE (Jenkinson 2003); SEGUE (Karsa 2019); BEST PATH (Abdul-Rahman 2007); ROMEO (Dymerska 2021); UMPIRE (Robinson 2014). Laplacian unwrapping is very robust even when highly wrapped, but its error has low spatial variability, which QSM removes with the background field anyway; where accuracy matters (shimming, qMRI) it is not recommended. PRELUDE-type unwrappers cannot resolve offsets larger than π between time points.

Multi-echo: fit the slope

  • Unwrap each echo spatially, then across echoes
  • Fit a line through all echoes
  • A 2π offset moves the line, not its slope

More echoes → more accurate maps (QSM, shimming)

Two unwrapped solutions of three echoes, exactly 2π apart.

Multi-echo (three or more) improves accuracy; all dual-echo pitfalls apply, plus later echoes need enough signal. The field map is the slope of phase against time (Eq. 5.9). Simplest pipeline: spatially unwrap every echo, then temporally unwrap to remove 2nπ offsets between echoes (needs Δφ < π between them), then a linear fit. Figure 5.15: two solutions 2π apart, e.g. from a different seed voxel; same slope, so the same field map. An advantage over phase-difference methods.

A close pair, then a late echo

  • TE1, TE2 close: no wraps, a first field estimate
  • It predicts the wraps of a late TE3
  • Refit with all three: more precise
  • Late echoes have less SNR: weight by magnitude
Figure 5.16: the first two echoes respect Nyquist and are unwrapped accurately; the third has a much longer TE. The first two predict the number of wraps in the third; with all three unwrapped, a three-point fit. Because σphase = 1/SNRmag, long echoes can hurt if not accounted for: weigh echoes by the SNR of their magnitude images.

UMPIRE

  • Three echoes, slightly uneven spacing: ΔTE1 ≠ ΔTE2
  • δTE = ΔTE2 − ΔTE1 is short: its phase cannot wrap
  • It predicts every wrap: ΔTEs can exceed the Nyquist limit

Click the legend to show or hide traces.

UMPIRE (Robinson 2014) only uses temporal unwrapping and needs at least three echoes. The two echo spacings are chosen slightly different; the phase accrued over their difference δTE is small and free of wraps, so it gives the field, which predicts and removes the wraps in each echo before a linear fit. Its phase must be below π but above zero. (The book writes the accrued phase over "TE2 − TE1 = δTE"; the small interval is the difference of the spacings.) Figure 5.17: one voxel unwrapped with UMPIRE although its phase changes by more than π between echoes.

UMPIRE and noise

  • δTE is short: a little noise is a large field error
  • Wrong wrap count → wrong slope
  • PRELUDE, SEGUE, ROMEO: less noise-sensitive, but no ΔTE beyond π

Slider: phase offset added to the 2nd echo.

Figure 5.18: the same data as Figure 5.17 with an offset added to the second echo, as noise would; watch the unwrapped points and the fit jump. Unwrappers such as PRELUDE are less susceptible to noise but cannot resolve phase changes larger than π between time points and can be slow; SEGUE is similar but faster; ROMEO is fast and performs better than PRELUDE and BEST PATH with noise.

Eddy currents and real-time mapping

Eddy currents

  • Fast gradient switching → fields that change with TE
  • They do not cancel in Δφ
  • Fixes: longer TR, one echo per TR, or reverse all gradients and average

Real-time (breathing)

  • A map in much less than a breath
  • 2D, slice by slice: each slice has its own time
  • Dual-echo GRE, minimum TR and TEs (EPI is fast, but distorted)
Eddy currents from rapidly changing gradients create spatially and temporally varying fields, which can differ between echo times and so do not cancel in the phase difference. Longer TRs help. For short TRs, acquire twice with frequency, phase and slice encoding reversed, which reverses the eddy-current polarity, and average the two field maps. Real-time mapping of respiration: acquire a map in much less than a respiratory cycle; 2D so slice timing can be associated with each slice's field; EPI is faster but distorted, so a 2D dual-echo GRE with minimum TR, short TEs and short RF pulses.

Key takeaways

  1. ΔB0 grows with B0 and is dominated by air–tissue interfaces
  2. Phase grows linearly with TE: φ = φ0 + γΔB0t, so two echoes at least
  3. ΔTE trades precision against wraps: no wraps if ΔTE < 1/(2Δfmax)
  4. Unwrapping (temporal or spatial) leaves a 2nπ ambiguity; singularities cannot be unwrapped
  5. More echoes: fit the slope; UMPIRE reaches long ΔTEs but is noise-sensitive
